The Rise of AI Agent Marketplaces: What Enterprises Need to Know

BK
Beehive Strategy · February 23, 2026 · 5 min read

The first wave of enterprise AI was about building models. The second wave was about building applications. The third wave — happening now — is about assembling pre-built agents into workflows. AI agent marketplaces are the infrastructure making this possible, and the enterprises that figure out how to use them well are compressing months of AI delivery into days.

What an AI Agent Marketplace Provides

A marketplace offers pre-built agents for common business functions: sales pipeline analysis, financial reporting, inventory optimisation, HR analytics. Each agent comes with predefined metrics, data source connectors, and conversation templates — reducing deployment time from months to days. The agent is not a demo; it is a working piece of the workflow with a defined job.

The economics follow the same logic that made app stores and API marketplaces work: the fixed cost of building a well-engineered agent is spread across thousands of enterprises, and each enterprise pays a fraction of what a bespoke build would cost. The counterintuitive part is that customisation still happens — every enterprise wires the agent to its own data, metrics, and governance — but the heavy lifting of prompt engineering, connector maintenance, and quality testing is done once, by the vendor, not N times by N customers.

Adoption data points in the same direction. Gartner has predicted that by 2028, 33% of enterprise software applications will include agentic AI, up from less than 1% in 2024 — a prediction that makes sense only if agents become packaged, purchasable, and integrable rather than bespoke engineering projects. Marketplaces are the packaging layer that makes that projection plausible.

The Build vs Buy vs Assemble Decision

Building every agent from scratch is expensive and slow. Buying a monolithic AI platform locks you into one vendor. Assembling pre-built agents from a marketplace — then customising for your specific needs — offers the best balance of speed, cost, and flexibility. McKinsey's work on automation suggests the prize is large: researchers there have estimated that roughly 60-70% of current work activities could be automated with generative and agentic AI, which means the assembly decision is being made at enterprise scale right now.

The build path fails on the math. A well-built, tested, governed agent is a serious engineering project — model selection, prompt design, evaluation, connectors, security, monitoring — and most enterprises need dozens of them. Teams that build everything discover the maintenance burden only after the first year, when every model upgrade and every data schema change ripples through every hand-built agent.

The buy path fails on fit. A monolithic platform's agents are designed for the vendor's median customer, and the median is nobody in particular. Integration with your data sources, your metric definitions, your approval workflows, and your compliance rules is where the real work lives — and a monolith gives you little room to do it your way.

Evaluating Marketplace Agents

Key criteria: does the agent connect to your data sources? Does it respect your governance policies (RBAC, audit trails)? Can it be customised without code? Is the underlying prompt logic transparent? Agents that can't answer 'yes' to all four are not enterprise-ready.

Transparency deserves special attention, because it is the criterion teams skip and regret. You need to know what the agent does with your data, which model it uses, what its evaluation results are, and how it handles the queries it cannot answer confidently. An agent that silently produces confident wrong answers is worse than no agent, so the marketplace's quality signals — evaluation results, version history, and the ability to test against your own questions before committing — are worth more than any feature list.

Connectors are the second make-or-break criterion. The agent's value is entirely downstream of whether it can reach your data through your security posture. A marketplace with first-class support for the systems you actually run — your warehouse, your CRM, your collaboration tools, via standard protocols like MCP — is far more valuable than one with fifty exotic connectors you will never use.

How Do You Choose a Marketplace?

Choose the marketplace you can leave. The decisive tests are portability and provenance: can you export your customisations, do agents interoperate with other tools through standard interfaces, and can you run the same workflow if you switch providers? A marketplace that locks in your workflow definitions is not a marketplace; it is a dependency with a nicer name.

  • Test with your own data and your own questions before committing; a quality agent must pass your golden set, not the vendor's demo.
  • Check governance integration: RBAC, audit trails, and data residency must map to your existing controls without custom engineering.
  • Verify customisation paths — configuration first, then code — so business teams can adapt agents without a backlog slot.
  • Confirm agent-to-agent interoperability through standard protocols (MCP) rather than proprietary glue.
  • Negotiate exportability of workflows and configurations from day one; test it before you depend on it.

The same evaluation discipline that applies to picking a single agent applies to picking a marketplace, with one addition: observe the ecosystem's velocity. How often do agents get updated, how fast do new connectors appear, and how quickly are vulnerabilities patched? In a fast-moving market, a stagnant marketplace is a depreciation schedule with a login page.

Governance, Security, and Agent Provenance

Agent marketplaces change the security conversation because they move code and behaviour across organisational boundaries. Every agent you adopt is third-party logic operating on your data, which means provenance — knowing who built it, what it was tested against, and how it is versioned — becomes a core governance requirement, not a procurement nicety.

Operationally, three controls cover most of the risk. First, run new agents in a sandboxed evaluation environment against representative data before production access. Second, enforce least-privilege data access at the agent level, so an agent can reach only what its function requires — a sales analytics agent does not need access to HR data, and the platform should make that separation structural, not aspirational. Third, keep audit trails of what each agent did, which version ran, and what it touched, so a bad result can be traced to a specific agent version and rolled back cleanly.

The governance bar is exactly why enterprise-ready marketplaces are winning and consumer-style 'browse and install' marketplaces are stalling inside companies. An enterprise will tolerate a slower catalogue if every entry ships with provenance, evaluation, and versioning; it will not tolerate the reverse. The marketplace that treats governance as a feature will win the enterprise segment.

The Future: Composable AI

The trajectory is clear: enterprises will compose workflows from multiple specialised agents — a sales agent feeds a forecasting agent feeds a procurement agent. Marketplaces that support agent-to-agent communication and orchestration will win. Those that treat agents as isolated tools will lose.

Composability changes the unit of architecture. Instead of 'the AI system', enterprises will have agent graphs — networks of specialised agents passing structured outputs to each other under orchestration, with humans in the loop at decision points. The value compounds because each agent improves independently: upgrade the forecasting agent and every workflow that consumes it improves without a rebuild.

The winning position in this world is owning the orchestration layer and the semantic layer — the common definitions of metrics and data that every agent shares — rather than owning any single model or agent. That is why the marketplaces built around a shared semantic foundation are the ones to watch: they give enterprises the ability to swap components without rewriting meaning, which is the definition of an architecture that can survive the pace of change in AI.

Key Takeaways

  • Agent marketplaces package AI into purchasable, integrable components — compressing deployment from months to days.
  • Assemble rather than build or buy wholesale: marketplace agents, customised with your data and governance.
  • Evaluate agents on data access, governance fit, no-code customisation, and prompt transparency — all four are non-negotiable.
  • Choose a marketplace you can leave: standard protocols, exportable workflows, and visible ecosystem velocity.
  • Treat provenance, least-privilege access, and audit trails as core requirements; agent security is a governance problem, not a procurement checkbox.

Conclusion

The rise of agent marketplaces marks the point where enterprise AI stopped being a build project and became an assembly discipline. The enterprises ahead are not the ones with the best models; they are the ones with the best workflows, composed from proven components and governed with the same rigour they apply to any third-party software.

For conversational BI specifically, the marketplace model is already the most practical path. Pre-built analytics agents over a shared semantic layer, delivered as a managed service and deployed in two weeks — which is how Beehive Strategy brings IM-native conversational BI to enterprises — show what assembly looks like when the underlying meaning of the data is governed once and reused everywhere. That combination of packaged capability and shared semantics is the pattern the next decade of enterprise AI will run on.
