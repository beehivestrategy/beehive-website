How to structure your team for AI vendor and model selection

In short · answer first

Assemble a dedicated, cross-functional team with clear decision rights. This team must operate on a continuous evaluation cycle, not as a one-off project. Their mandate is to assess technical viability, commercial terms, and operational readiness against a predefined framework to de-risk adoption and ensure strategic alignment.

Key takeaways
  • Establish a central AI vendor council with delegated authority
  • Define clear RACI matrices for evaluation and onboarding
  • Mandate a standardised vendor assessment framework
  • Implement a continuous model performance review cycle

The core cross-functional team structure

Your first action is to charter a dedicated AI Vendor Council. This is not a committee but a decision-making body with delegated authority from the C-suite. Its primary function is to govern the entire vendor and model selection lifecycle, from initial sourcing to production deployment and ongoing performance management. This prevents fragmented, siloed decisions.

The council must be cross-functional, with a core of six key roles. These include a technical lead from MLOps, a data governance officer, a procurement specialist, a security architect, a business domain product owner, and a legal representative for compliance. This composition ensures all critical facets of a vendor engagement are scrutinised from the outset.

Each member must have dedicated time allocated to this function, typically thirty to fifty percent of their capacity. This is non-negotiable. Treating vendor evaluation as an ancillary duty leads to rushed assessments and oversight gaps. The council requires an executive sponsor to resolve blockers and secure budget for its operations.

Beyond the core team, establish a formal escalation path and a lightweight advisory council. The escalation path should pre-define the specific issues (e.g., a major security finding, a significant budget overrun, an ethical concern) that require immediate elevation to a C-level executive or steering committee for a final decision. The advisory council, comprising senior leaders from finance, legal, and operations, should convene quarterly. Their role is not in day-to-day selection but in providing strategic alignment, ensuring the team's efforts support overarching business goals, and championing the initiative across the organization to secure broad buy-in and remove institutional roadblocks.

Defining roles, responsibilities, and decision rights

Clarity of decision rights is paramount to avoid paralysis. Utilise a RACI matrix (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) to document every step of the evaluation process. For instance, the technical lead is Responsible for benchmarking model performance, while the council is Accountable for the final selection decision.

The business product owner defines the problem statement and success metrics, ensuring any solution aligns with user needs. The procurement and legal leads own the commercial and contractual viability, negotiating terms that protect your organisation’s data and intellectual property. This separation of duties prevents conflicts of interest.

Establish a clear voting mechanism for the council. A simple majority may suffice for low-risk engagements, but mandate unanimous consensus for high-stakes decisions involving sensitive data or critical business processes. Document these decision rules in the council’s charter to ensure consistent and defensible outcomes.

To prevent ambiguity, codify decision rights using a RACI matrix for key milestones. For instance, the Technical Lead is Responsible for running proof-of-concept tests, the Business Lead is Accountable for signing off on the business requirements being met, the Legal/Compliance role must be Consulted on data processing agreements, and the entire core team is Informed of the final recommendation. Furthermore, establish clear decision rules upfront, such as 'Any vendor failing a critical security requirement is automatically disqualified' or 'Final selection requires a consensus from the Business and Technical leads, with other roles providing advisory votes.' This creates a transparent, rules-based process.

The vendor assessment framework and scorecard

Do not evaluate vendors on ad-hoc criteria. Develop a standardised assessment framework that all contenders are measured against. This framework should be organised into four primary pillars: Technical Capability, Commercial Terms, Security and Compliance, and Operational Viability. This ensures a holistic and comparable evaluation.

Within each pillar, define specific, weighted criteria. For example, under Technical Capability, you might score inference latency, model accuracy on your data, and fine-tuning capabilities. Under Operational Viability, assess the vendor’s support SLAs, documentation quality, and disaster recovery protocols. Weightings reflect your organisation’s strategic priorities.

Operationalise this framework through a quantitative scorecard. This artefact forces objective comparison and reduces bias. Each council member scores their respective section, and the scores are aggregated for a total. This data-driven approach provides a clear audit trail justifying why one vendor was selected over another.

The scorecard should break down abstract criteria into measurable, evidence-based questions. Instead of 'Good scalability,' ask 'Does the vendor provide documented auto-scaling capabilities and what are the associated cost tiers?' or 'Can you provide a recent load test report simulating a 5x traffic increase?' For model performance, go beyond basic accuracy. Include specific metrics like inference latency (p95 under 200ms), a fairness/bias assessment against key demographic segments, and a measure of drift over time. Assign weighted scores to each line item, forcing the team to quantitatively debate the trade-offs between a vendor with superior technical specs but a higher cost versus a more affordable but less scalable option.

The continuous evaluation operating rhythm

Vendor management is not a project with an end date; it is a continuous function. The council must establish a regular operating rhythm. This includes monthly deep-dive reviews for existing strategic vendors and quarterly horizon-scanning sessions to assess new market entrants and model releases.

For each production-deployed model, institute a formal performance review cycle. This should occur monthly initially, moving to quarterly once stable. The review must analyse operational metrics like uptime and cost, but also model-specific metrics like accuracy drift and business KPIs. This identifies degradation early.

Maintain a vendor registry, a living document cataloguing all engagements, their status, key contacts, and renewal dates. This single source of truth prevents shadow IT and ensures the council has full visibility into all AI-related expenditures and dependencies across the enterprise.

Formalize the evaluation cadence with a standard agenda and pre-defined triggers. The monthly operational review should examine system health dashboards, tracking metrics like API error rates, latency, and cost-per-prediction against baselines. The biannual strategic review is for deeper analysis: re-running model performance benchmarks on fresh data, re-assessing the competitive landscape for new entrants, and conducting a full cost-benefit analysis. Crucially, establish clear performance triggers that mandate an ad-hoc review, such as a 15% degradation in model accuracy, a significant change in data distribution, or a major security vulnerability announced in a core framework the model depends upon.

Key artefacts and deliverables for governance

The council’s work must be captured in standardised artefacts to ensure repeatability and knowledge retention. The primary deliverable for any new vendor is a Vendor Assessment Dossier. This comprehensive document contains the completed scorecard, a risk register, the commercial quote, and the implementation roadmap.

Another critical artefact is the Model Card for any procured model. This document, inspired by industry best practice, details the model’s intended use, performance characteristics, training data origins, and known limitations. It is essential for transparent governance and safe deployment.

For approved vendors, the council must produce a simplified Business Readiness Brief for executive stakeholders. This one-page summary outlines the business case, risk assessment, and implementation plan, facilitating swift sign-off. This streamlines the procurement process while maintaining rigorous oversight.

A critical artefact is the Model Card, a standardized one-page document for each approved model. This card summarizes key information for stakeholders: its intended use and limitations, the data it was trained on, performance metrics across different subgroups, and its known failure modes. Another essential deliverable is the Decision Log. This is a living document that records every significant choice, the rationale behind it, and the individuals involved. For example, an entry might state: 'Date: 10/26/2023. Decision: Selected Vendor A over Vendor B for superior explainability features, despite a 10% higher cost. Rationale: Regulatory compliance requires full audit trails. Approved by: Jane Doe (Business Lead), John Smith (Tech Lead).'

Integrating with existing procurement and IT governance

The AI Vendor Council cannot operate as an island; it must integrate with established corporate functions. Its charter should explicitly define its interaction with the central procurement office. The council sets the technical and security requirements, while procurement executes the negotiation and contracting based on that mandate.

Similarly, integrate with the IT governance board. The council’s vendor recommendations should follow a formal gated process for technical and security architecture review before final approval. This ensures new AI tools adhere to enterprise infrastructure standards and security policies, preventing future integration debt.

This integration is formalised through shared membership. Your procurement lead on the council is the liaison to the broader procurement function. Your security architect is the conduit to the CISO’s organisation. This embedded model ensures efficiency and alignment with corporate policies.

To ensure smooth integration, the AI team must map its process onto the existing IT governance lifecycle. This means the AI project proposal should be submitted through the standard IT project intake portal, and the selected AI vendor must undergo the official enterprise security review conducted by the CISO's team. The AI team's Proof-of-Concept (POC) report becomes the technical feasibility input for the broader IT governance board. Furthermore, the AI model itself should be treated as a software asset, requiring registration in the corporate IT asset management system and adherence to all standard patch and vulnerability management policies that apply to other critical software components.

Budgeting and resourcing the function

The council requires a dedicated budget for its operations. This covers tooling for model benchmarking, potential costs for proof-of-concept environments, and subscriptions to industry analyst reports. Without a budget, the team lacks the resources to conduct thorough due diligence.

Beyond the council itself, budget must be allocated for the ongoing total cost of ownership of vendor models. This includes not only licensing fees but also the cloud compute costs for inference, the internal personnel costs for integration and maintenance, and potential costs for data egress or fine-tuning.

Justify this investment by framing it as risk mitigation. The cost of a poorly vetted vendor—through a security incident, compliance failure, or operational outage—far exceeds the operational budget of the council. This function is a strategic investment in de-risking the organisation’s AI adoption.

Budgeting must account for both direct and hidden costs. Direct costs include vendor licensing fees, cloud computing/inference costs, and any dedicated training data acquisition. Crucially, budget for hidden operational costs: the fractional time of core team members (e.g., 20% of a data scientist's time for monitoring), potential costs for external audit services, and contingency funds for fine-tuning or retraining the model if performance drifts. From a resourcing perspective, plan for a tiered support model. The core team handles strategic vendor relationships and major incidents, while Level 1 support for common user issues and model API errors is integrated into the existing IT service desk, equipped with a specific troubleshooting checklist for the AI system.

Scaling the model across business units

For a large enterprise, a single central council can become a bottleneck. A hub-and-spoke model is often more effective. The central council sets the strategy, framework, and policies, while delegated councils in major business units or regions handle evaluations for their specific domain needs.

The central council maintains oversight by reviewing all high-risk engagements and conducting audits of business unit decisions. It also manages the enterprise-wide vendor registry and negotiates centralised master agreements with strategic vendors to leverage economies of scale.

This scaled model ensures governance and consistency while empowering business units to move with agility. It requires a clear delegation of authority matrix and regular syncs between central and business unit councils to share learnings and maintain alignment on the overall AI strategy.

Scaling requires a centralized enablement function and a lightweight governance model for business units (BUs). Establish a Center of Excellence (CoE) that develops standardized playbooks, contract templates, and the master vendor scorecard that BUs can customize. The CoE also maintains a central registry of all AI models deployed across the company to prevent duplication of effort. For each BU, mandate that they form their own lightweight version of the core team, following the same cross-functional structure. Their autonomy is bounded by a gating process; any model with high-risk implications, a budget over a defined threshold, or using personal data must be reviewed and approved by the central CoE to ensure enterprise-wide standards are consistently met.
