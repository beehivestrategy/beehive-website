In short · answer first

To prove generative AI vendor outcomes, shift from technical metrics to business value. Establish a baseline, define specific business KPIs, and implement a controlled measurement plan. Rigorously attribute results to isolate the AI's impact, providing the evidence needed for continued investment and scaling.

Key takeaways
  • Establish a pre-implementation performance baseline for comparison
  • Define specific business KPIs, not just technical metrics
  • Implement a controlled measurement plan for clear attribution
  • Calculate the total economic impact to prove financial value

Move Beyond Technical Metrics to Business Value

Enterprise buyers often fall into the trap of evaluating generative AI vendors solely on technical benchmarks like tokens per second or model accuracy. These metrics, while important for engineering teams, do not translate to the boardroom. Your primary task is to reframe the conversation around tangible business outcomes, such as reduced operational costs, accelerated time-to-market, or improved customer satisfaction scores.

The first strategic shift requires defining what success looks like for your specific business function. For a customer support centre, success might be a 25% reduction in average handling time. For a legal department, it could be a 40% decrease in contract review durations. This moves the evaluation from abstract technical performance to concrete operational and financial impact that resonates with executive stakeholders.

This business-value approach necessitates close collaboration between the procurement team, the business unit leading the implementation, and finance. Together, they must establish a clear line of sight from the vendor's capability to a key performance indicator that matters to the organisation. This alignment is your foundational step for any meaningful measurement.

To operationalize this shift, the procurement team should mandate that vendors provide detailed Value Realization Roadmaps. These artifacts must map specific AI capabilities to departmental KPIs, such as a 15% reduction in first-draft time for marketing content or a 20% decrease in Level 1 support ticket volume. Assign an AI Product Owner within the business unit to validate these projections against current operational baselines. This role is responsible for ensuring the proposed metrics are not just technically feasible but are also aligned with strategic objectives like customer satisfaction (CSAT) scores or employee productivity, moving the conversation from model accuracy to measurable business outcomes.

Establish a Rigorous Pre-Implementation Baseline

You cannot prove an outcome without knowing your starting point. Before any vendor integration begins, you must capture a detailed snapshot of current performance. This baseline must be measured over a sufficient period, typically 30 to 90 days, to account for normal operational variance and establish statistical significance for later comparison.

The baseline should directly mirror the business KPIs you defined for success. If your goal is improved developer productivity, measure the current average story points delivered per sprint. If it is content creation, record the hours spent drafting marketing copy. This data must be granular, reliable, and documented in a format that allows for unambiguous post-implementation comparison.

This process often reveals a critical gap: many organisations lack the systems to easily capture this operational data. Assign a data analyst from your team to work with the business unit to instrument the necessary workflows. This investment in measurement infrastructure is non-negotiable for proving value later.

The baseline analysis must be a formal document, co-signed by department heads. For a customer service application, this involves extracting a representative sample of, for instance, 5,000 support tickets from the previous quarter. The analysis should catalog the average handle time (AHT), resolution rate, and escalations for this sample. This creates an immutable pre-AI benchmark. Crucially, the process owner must also quantify the fully-loaded cost of these activities, including agent time, software licensing, and overhead. This financial baseline is the critical numerator for calculating future ROI and prevents post-implementation debates about the starting point.

Design a Controlled Measurement Plan for Attribution

Attribution is the most common failure point in proving AI value. To confidently claim that the vendor's tool caused an improvement, you must control for other variables. The most robust method is an A/B test, where a control group continues business-as-usual processes while a pilot group uses the new AI solution.

For functions where a full A/B test is operationally difficult, adopt a phased rollout strategy. Measure the entire function's performance pre-rollout, then measure the same KPIs as you deploy the solution to discrete teams or regions sequentially. This allows you to compare the performance of adopted teams against those still waiting for deployment, strengthening your attribution argument.

Your measurement plan must also account for external factors. Did a seasonal demand spike affect results? Was there a change in market conditions? Document these factors during the measurement period. This rigorous approach to attribution provides the defensible data required to justify scaling the investment beyond the pilot phase.

A key artefact is a controlled pilot design document. For example, a legal department might deploy the AI tool to 50% of its associates for contract review, while the other 50% operates as a control group using standard methods. The measurement plan must define the exact data to be pulled from both groups’ workflow systems for the duration of the pilot (e.g., 90 days). This includes time spent per contract, error rates identified by senior review, and redline volume. A business analyst should be tasked with collecting this data weekly to ensure a clean, attributable comparison that isolates the AI's impact from other variables.

Calculate the Total Economic Impact

Ultimately, your CFO will demand a clear financial narrative. Technical wins must be translated into pounds and pence. Build a total economic impact (TEI) model that quantifies both the benefits and the costs associated with the vendor partnership. This moves the discussion from qualitative praise to quantitative proof.

The benefit side of the model must include hard cost savings, such as reduced licence fees for displaced software or lower labour costs. Crucially, it must also quantify soft benefits, such as the value of employee time reinvested into higher-value tasks. Assign a conservative monetary value to these productivity gains based on fully burdened labour rates.

Offset these benefits against the full cost of ownership: subscription fees, implementation costs, internal labour for management, and any additional cloud infrastructure spend. The resulting net positive value, payback period, and return on investment become your ultimate validation signals for the procurement category and the specific vendor choice.

Extend the TEI analysis beyond direct cost savings to include risk mitigation and opportunity cost. For instance, quantify the financial impact of reducing compliance violations through more consistent AI-generated reporting, perhaps using historical fine data as a reference. Additionally, calculate the opportunity cost of reallocating skilled employees from repetitive tasks to high-value work. If an engineer saves 10 hours a week on documentation, assign a dollar value to the innovation projects they can now pursue. This comprehensive view often reveals that the strategic value of generative AI significantly outweighs the direct operational savings, justifying a larger investment.

Continuously Monitor and Report on Vendor SLAs

Proving initial value is one thing; ensuring sustained performance is another. Your vendor agreement must include stringent service-level agreements (SLAs) that are tied to your business KPIs, not just uptime. These could include guarantees on output quality, latency for user-facing applications, or accuracy rates for specific tasks.

Establish a monthly vendor performance review, chaired by a dedicated programme manager. This meeting should review dashboards tracking the agreed SLAs against actual performance. It is also the forum to address model drift—the degradation of performance over time—and to ensure the vendor's roadmap continues to align with your evolving business needs.

This ongoing discipline turns a one-off validation into a continuous improvement process. It provides the data needed to negotiate contract renewals from a position of strength and holds the vendor accountable for delivering the long-term value they promised during the sales cycle.

Monitoring should be automated through a dedicated performance dashboard, not manual checks. Require the vendor to provide API access to real-time logs for uptime, latency, and output quality (e.g., factuality scores). The IT operations team must establish alert rules; for instance, if latency exceeds the SLA threshold for three consecutive hours, a ticket is automatically created and escalated. Furthermore, schedule monthly business reviews (MBRs) with the vendor to discuss not just SLA adherence but also trend analysis on usage patterns and user feedback, creating a continuous feedback loop for performance optimization and contract evolution.

Build a Compelling Business Case for Scaling

The final step is synthesising your measured outcomes into an irresistible business case for scaling the investment. This document should tell a clear story: here was our problem, here is the solution we piloted, here is the rigorous proof of its impact, and here is the financial case for broader deployment.

Structure the business case around the three pillars of value: financial return, operational improvement, and strategic advantage. Use visuals from your dashboards to show performance trends. Include testimonials from pilot group users to add qualitative colour to the quantitative data. This combination is powerful for persuading sceptical stakeholders.

Present this case to your investment committee with a clear roadmap for scaling, outlining the resources required, the timeline, and the expected incremental benefits. A well-measured pilot provides the confidence to make a much larger bet, transforming a tactical experiment into a strategic capability.

The final business case must present a clear go/no-go decision rule based on the pilot's outcomes. For example, 'If the pilot demonstrates a minimum of 10% productivity gain and a positive net present value (NPV) over three years, proceed with a phased enterprise rollout.' The artefact should be a multi-year rollout roadmap, detailing which business units will onboard in each quarter, the associated implementation costs, and the required headcount for a dedicated Center of Excellence (CoE) to provide governance and support. This transforms experimental results into an executable, funded strategy for scaling generative AI across the organization.
