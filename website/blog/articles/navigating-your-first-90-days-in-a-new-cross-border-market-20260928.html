Navigating Your First 90 Days in a New Cross-Border Market

In short · answer first

The first 90 days are about validating your initial hypothesis with local evidence, not executing a pre-set plan. Success hinges on securing three lighthouse customer contracts to prove product-market fit and establishing a minimal, compliant operational footprint to enable early delivery and build market credibility.

Key takeaways
  • Secure three local lighthouse customers within 90 days
  • Establish a minimal viable entity for legal compliance
  • Validate core value proposition with local user feedback
  • Build a 12-month localised financial model with scenarios

Week Zero: Establishing Your Initial Market Hypothesis

Before your first day, crystallise your cross-border hypothesis into a single-page document. This artefact, your Market Entry Canvas, must define your target customer profile, your unique value proposition for that specific geography, and your three highest-probability channels for initial lead generation. Avoid generic statements; specificity is your compass. For example, target 'directors at Singaporean fintechs with 50-200 employees' rather than 'ASEAN financial services'.

Assign clear roles for this pre-launch phase. The commercial lead owns the customer profile and lighthouse target list. The product lead defines the minimum viable product (MVP) features for this new market. The operations lead identifies the three most critical regulatory hurdles. This document is your foundational thesis, but you must treat it as a assumption to be tested, not a plan to be rigidly executed.

Convene a formal hypothesis review with your leadership team. The decision rule to proceed is a unanimous 'go' based on the canvas. If any lead cannot sign off on their respective section, the launch is paused. This gates investment and ensures your entire team enters the new market with a aligned, albeit preliminary, understanding of the opportunity and its inherent risks.

To formalise your initial market hypothesis, create a one-page validation canvas. This artefact should clearly state your target customer segment, their primary pain point, your proposed value proposition, and the key regulatory or logistical assumptions that underpin your entry. Assign a team member to own each assumption and define the specific data points—such as customer interview transcripts, competitor pricing sheets, or regulatory agency guidance documents—required to validate or invalidate it within the first 30 days. This canvas becomes your team's single source of truth and a living document for the first quarter.

Days 1-30: Activating Local Networks and Securing Pilots

Your entire first month is dedicated to conversation, not conversion. The primary goal is to book 30 qualified meetings with potential buyers, partners, and regulatory advisors. Your commercial lead must be on the ground, leveraging platforms like LinkedIn Sales Navigator with hyper-localised messaging. The key metric is meeting attendance rate; a sub-60% rate signals a flawed value proposition or targeting model.

In each meeting, lead with curiosity, not a sales pitch. Use a standardised set of open-ended questions to test the assumptions on your Market Entry Canvas. Your product lead should join to hear firsthand the language customers use to describe their problems. Document every conversation in your CRM with a specific tag for this market initiative to track signal versus noise.

By day 30, you must have secured verbal agreements for two or three pilot projects with lighthouse customers. These are not full contracts but memorandums of understanding (MOUs) that define a 60-day evaluation period. The decision rule for a pilot is a customer commitment of key personnel time and access to necessary data, proving a willingness to collaborate.

A critical but often overlooked role in this phase is the 'Local Navigator'. This should be a trusted, well-connected in-country consultant or early hire whose primary KPI is scheduling 15-20 qualified introductory meetings with potential pilot partners, channel distributors, and legal advisors within the first three weeks. Their network is your most valuable asset. Provide them with a structured interview script focused on understanding local business etiquette, payment term expectations, and competitor perceptions to ensure every interaction yields actionable intelligence, not just a new contact.

When securing pilots, insist on a jointly agreed-upon 'Success Definition Memo' with each partner. This one-page document should outline the pilot's objectives, key performance indicators (e.g., user acquisition cost, conversion rate), respective responsibilities, data-sharing protocols, and clear go/no-go decision points for Day 30. This prevents misaligned expectations and transforms subjective feedback into objective, data-driven decisions about whether to proceed, pivot, or abandon a partnership.

Building Your Minimal Viable Entity for Operations

Concurrently, your operations lead must establish your minimal viable entity. This is not a full subsidiary but the legal and financial structure necessary to invoice clients, pay local expenses, and remain compliant. In many markets, this involves engaging a local corporate services firm to establish a representative office or a similar lightweight structure within weeks, not months.

The core artefacts are a local business bank account, a standard service agreement vetted by a local law firm, and a clear understanding of your tax obligations, particularly value-added tax (VAT) or goods and services tax (GST). The decision rule for any operational process is necessity: if you cannot invoice a customer or pay a consultant without it, it is a priority-one task.

Avoid the common pitfall of over-investing in office space or full-time hires. Initially, leverage a registered agent address and a co-working space for meetings. Hire a local, part-time finance administrator through a specialised recruitment agency to manage accounts and compliance filings. This keeps fixed costs low while enabling essential operations.

The MVE must be designed for compliance from day one. This necessitates appointing a 'Compliance Lead' (often an external legal firm) to establish a mandatory checklist. Key items include registering for VAT/GST, understanding employee vs. contractor classification laws, setting up local payroll processing, and ensuring data storage complies with local privacy regulations (like GDPR in Europe or PIPL in China). The decision rule is simple: no operational activity, especially those handling customer data or money, can commence without sign-off from this checklist to mitigate severe regulatory risk.

Days 31-60: From Pilot Validation to Initial Revenue

This phase is dedicated to delivering exceptional value within the pilot engagements. Assign your best delivery manager to oversee these first projects, ensuring they run smoothly and on schedule. The primary goal is to generate a quantifiable result for the pilot customer—a specific percentage efficiency gain or cost reduction—that can be referenced in future case studies.

Formally gather feedback at the pilot's mid-point and conclusion. Use a structured survey to measure net promoter score (NPS) and product-market fit (PMF). A PMF score below 40% for your core offering is a major red flag requiring immediate strategy reassessment. This feedback is your most valuable data for iterating your value proposition.

Convert successful pilots into your first paying contracts. The commercial lead negotiates these initial deals, which may be at a discounted rate but must transition to standard pricing within six months. The signing of the first three contracts is the definitive signal that you have achieved initial product-market fit and can scale your investment.

The transition from pilot to revenue requires a defined monetisation approval process. Create a 'Pricing Committee' comprising the Head of Sales, Finance Lead, and Country Manager. Their mandate is to review pilot data and approve a final localised pricing tier based on two key metrics: Customer Lifetime Value (LTV) and Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC). A worked example: if your pilot shows a CAC of €100 and an expected LTV of €300, the 1:3 ratio supports a sustainable model. If CAC nears or exceeds LTV, the committee must pivot the pricing or acquisition strategy immediately.

Localising Your Core Financial Model

By day 60, your finance director must localise your financial model. The initial model based on home-market assumptions is now obsolete. Integrate real local data: effective tax rates, typical salary bands for key roles, costs from local vendors, and foreign exchange hedging costs provided by your bank.

Build three scenarios: a baseline using your current pipeline, an upside case factoring in partner-led lead generation, and a downside case assuming longer sales cycles. The model must clearly show your cash runway under each scenario and your path to profitability. The decision rule for further investment is a baseline scenario that shows a positive gross margin within 18 months.

This model becomes your primary tool for monthly performance reviews. Track actual revenue, customer acquisition cost, and operational expenditure against the model's forecasts. A variance of more than 20% in any key metric for two consecutive months triggers a formal strategic review to understand the root cause and adjust the plan.

Localisation goes beyond currency conversion. The finance team must build a model that incorporates country-specific line items. For example, in Brazil, account for high credit card transaction fees (often 4-5%) and complex tax implications like ISS and COFINS. In Germany, model for solidariszuschlag (surcharge) tax and mandatory trade fee contributions (e.g., IHK). A key decision rule: if localised operational costs, including these hidden financial burdens, erode the net profit margin below a predetermined threshold (e.g., 15%), it should trigger an immediate review of the entire market entry strategy.

Days 61-90: Solidifying Your Market Position

The final month is about solidifying your foundation for scaling. With three lighthouse customers secured, shift marketing efforts to developing a localised case study for each. These one-page documents, highlighting specific results and customer testimonials, are your most powerful assets for building credibility with the broader market and attracting larger accounts.

Formalise your most successful channel partnerships. If a particular advisor or agency was instrumental in securing early meetings, draft a formal referral agreement. If a technology partnership proved critical for delivery, establish a joint value proposition and co-marketing plan. Systematise what worked organically in the first 60 days.

Conduct a formal 90-day review with the entire team. Revisit your original Market Entry Canvas and grade each assumption as confirmed, invalidated, or inconclusive. This review determines your strategy for the next quarter: a 'go' decision to scale the team and investment, a 'pivot' to adjust the target customer or proposition, or a rare 'no-go' to exit.

Solidifying your position requires a formalised customer feedback loop. Implement a quarterly 'Voice of the Customer' (VoC) programme managed by a product or marketing lead. This involves conducting 10-15 structured debrief calls with your first paying customers, using a standardised questionnaire to rate product-market fit on a scale of 1-10 and to identify the top three requested features. This qualitative data, when combined with usage metrics, provides the evidence needed to prioritise the product roadmap for the next quarter and justify further investment.

The Critical Governance Artefacts for the First Quarter

Maintain a single source of truth: the Market Entry Dashboard. This shared document, updated weekly, tracks your five key performance indicators against the 90-day targets. It must include the lighthouse target list, pipeline value, meeting count, operational milestone status, and cash burn. This ensures complete transparency and alignment across the entire leadership team.

Institute a weekly cross-functional meeting, the 'Market Entry Stand-up'. This 30-minute session is for reviewing the dashboard, resolving blockers, and assigning tasks for the coming week. The commercial, product, and operations leads must attend. The standing agenda is: pipeline review, operational hurdles, and customer feedback. No other topics are permitted.

The final artefact is the 90-Day Review Presentation. This is a formal document presented to the board or executive committee. It details the validated learning, the financial performance against plan, the state of the pipeline, and a clear recommendation for the path forward, supported by the localised financial model and customer case studies.

Beyond standard reports, institute a 'Strategic Decision Log'. This artefact, maintained by the Country Manager, records every significant decision made in the quarter, the rationale behind it, the alternatives considered, and the expected outcome. For example: 'Day 45: Decision to partner with Distributor A over B. Rationale: A offered better payment terms (net 60 vs. net 30) and owned a larger sales force. Expected outcome: 25% lower customer acquisition cost.' This creates institutional knowledge, aids in future reviews, and is invaluable for auditors.

Anticipating and Mitigating Common First-Quarter Risks

The most common risk is a prolonged sales cycle. Mitigate this by prioritising prospects with a clear budget and a recognised pain point. The decision rule after 30 days is to disqualify any prospect that cannot define a decision process within 90 days. Do not let a long-cycle opportunity distract from securing quick wins.

Regulatory ambiguity is a constant threat. To mitigate, engage a local compliance consultant on a retainer for the first six months. Their role is to provide weekly updates on regulatory changes and pre-vet your customer contracts and marketing materials. This is a non-negotiable cost of entry for regulated sectors.

Talent acquisition often takes longer than anticipated. Begin building a pipeline for your first local country manager and sales lead by day 60, even if you do not plan to hire until the next quarter. Use a specialised recruitment firm with a proven track record in your sector to avoid costly mis-hires.

A common risk is cultural misalignment in negotiations. To mitigate, develop a 'Negotiation Playbook' with guidance from your Local Navigator. This should detail local norms: for instance, in Japan, building trust (nemawashi) is essential before any deal, while in Israel, direct and challenging discussions are the norm. Include role-specific advice, such as whether the senior-most person is expected to speak first. A simple decision rule: if a key negotiation stalls twice due to cultural missteps, mandate that the Local Navigator joins all subsequent meetings to facilitate.
