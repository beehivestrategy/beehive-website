In short · answer first

The most critical factor is the consultancy's operating model, specifically its team composition and integration process. Look for a dedicated core team with clear roles, not just a list of technical experts. Assess their methodology for embedding with your staff and transferring knowledge to ensure sustainable outcomes beyond the initial deployment.

Key takeaways
  • Demand a named core team with explicit roles and senior oversight
  • Evaluate their client integration and knowledge transfer methodology
  • Require evidence of multi-disciplinary teams for production systems

Look beyond the technical CVs to the core team structure

Enterprise buyers often receive impressive technical CVs during vendor evaluation, but these rarely reveal the actual team structure. The proposed lead data scientist may be shared across six clients, and the promised senior strategist might only be available for quarterly reviews. You must interrogate the proposed operating model to understand who will be dedicated to your account, their specific roles, and their level of commitment.

Request a named core team with explicitly defined roles: a delivery lead accountable for outcomes, a technical architect for system design, and a data scientist for model development. This core team should be your primary point of contact and remain consistent for the project's duration. Avoid vendors who cannot guarantee this stability, as frequent team changes disrupt momentum and increase project risk significantly.

Confirm the seniority and engagement model of the strategic oversight. A partner or director should be actively involved, not just listed on a proposal. They must participate in key milestone reviews and be accountable for the project's strategic alignment. This ensures the team's work consistently drives towards your business objectives, not just technical completion.

Scrutinise the team's composition for a dedicated project manager or scrum master. This role is a critical indicator of their operational maturity, as it shifts the burden of project coordination from your internal staff or the technical lead. A team lacking this role often signals a 'body shop' approach, where you are simply renting individual contributors. Conversely, a dedicated facilitator ensures timelines are met, blockers are cleared, and communication is structured, allowing the technical experts to focus on solving complex AI problems rather than administrative tasks.

Request to review artefacts from their team onboarding process. A mature consultancy will have standardised playbooks for knowledge transfer, environment setup, and access provisioning. These documents demonstrate a repeatable methodology for integration, reducing the friction and time-to-productivity typically associated with bringing external teams up to speed. The absence of such materials suggests an ad-hoc, reactive approach that will consume valuable internal resources during the critical initial project phases.

Assess their model for integrating with your internal teams

A forward-deployed consultancy's value lies in its ability to embed within your organisation, not work in isolation. Their proposed operating model must detail how they will integrate with your internal technology, data, and business teams. Vague promises of 'collaboration' are insufficient; you need a concrete plan for daily interaction and decision-making processes.

Seek a clear methodology for knowledge transfer and upskilling your staff. This often involves paired programming sessions, co-creation workshops, and detailed documentation handovers. The consultancy should view your team's growing capability as a key success metric, not a threat to their ongoing engagement. This approach builds internal ownership and ensures the solution remains viable long after they depart.

Evaluate their tools and practices for seamless collaboration. Do they use communication platforms your team already operates, like Microsoft Teams or Slack? How do they manage version control and ensure your engineers have access to all artefacts? Their workflow must minimise disruption and foster a truly integrated unit, rather than creating a separate silo that reports back periodically.

Probe for their standard integration artefacts, specifically a RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) chart tailored for AI projects. This chart should clearly delineate decision rights for model approval, data access, and deployment sign-off between their team and your internal stakeholders (e.g., Data Governance, IT Security, Business Unit leads). A vague or non-existent RACI is a red flag for future conflicts and accountability gaps, often leading to project delays as teams struggle to determine who has the authority to make critical calls.

Evaluate their proposed communication cadence beyond weekly status meetings. Request examples of their typical dashboards or report formats. Look for evidence of metrics that matter for AI initiatives, such as model performance drift, data pipeline health, and business value tracking, rather than just task completion. A team that prioritises these value-centric communications demonstrates an understanding that AI projects are iterative scientific endeavours, not just software development tick-lists.

Demand evidence of multi-disciplinary production experience

Building a proof-of-concept is fundamentally different from deploying a production-grade AI system. Many consultancies excel at the former but lack the multi-disciplinary muscle for the latter. Your evaluation must probe their experience with the full lifecycle, including MLOps, data engineering, security hardening, and change management.

Ask for case studies that detail the entire team composition, not just the data scientists. A production-ready team includes ML engineers for deployment, data engineers for pipeline management, and DevOps specialists for infrastructure. The absence of these roles indicates a consultancy that prototypes but does not operationalise, leaving you with an unmaintainable asset.

Scrutinise their approach to non-functional requirements like scalability, security, and regulatory compliance. These are often handled by specialised roles within a team. Their operating model should explicitly account for how these concerns are addressed from day one, not as an afterthought. This foresight is what separates strategic partners from tactical vendors.

Demand to see a specific example of a Model Card or a similar ethics and fairness audit document from a past project. This artefact proves the team operationalises responsible AI principles beyond mere lip service. It should detail the model's intended use cases, limitations, performance across different subgroups, and the steps taken to mitigate bias. The ability to produce this document shows a ingrained practice of multi-disciplinary oversight, likely involving legal, compliance, or ethics specialists alongside the data scientists.

Ask for a case study detailing a production model failure and their response protocol. The narrative should explicitly mention the roles involved: did the data engineer debug the feature pipeline, the ML engineer roll back the model, and the product manager communicate with end-users? A team with genuine production experience will have a blameless post-mortem process that focuses on systemic fixes, providing a clear example of their multi-disciplinary coordination under pressure.

Map their operating model to your project's risk profile

Every AI project carries inherent risk, and the consultancy's operating model should be tailored to mitigate it. For a high-stakes, regulatory-heavy project, you need a team with strong governance and compliance expertise embedded within it. A more experimental initiative might prioritise rapid iteration and a lighter governance structure.

Analyse how their model allocates responsibility for risk management. Is there a dedicated role for tracking model drift and performance degradation post-deployment? Who is accountable for the ethical implications of the AI system? The operating model must have clear answers to these questions, with defined owners for each category of risk.

Consider the consultancy's scalability model. Can they rapidly augment the team with specific expertise if an unexpected challenge arises? Conversely, can they efficiently scale down certain roles as the project moves from build to run? A flexible operating model indicates maturity and a focus on your efficiency, not just their resource utilisation.

For a high-risk project (e.g., patient diagnostics), your evaluation must verify their quality assurance (QA) protocol. Ask if their ML engineers work alongside dedicated QA testers who develop adversarial test cases specifically designed to break the model under edge conditions. This specialised testing, focused on model robustness and edge-case failure modes, is a distinct discipline from standard software QA and is a non-negotiable for critical applications to prevent harmful outcomes.

Request their checklist for a pre-deployment 'go/no-go' meeting. For a low-risk, high-experimentation project, this checklist might be lightweight, focusing on data lineage and cost controls. For a high-stakes deployment, the checklist must be rigorous, requiring sign-off from their lead data scientist, your legal counsel, and a fairness auditor. This tangible artefact directly maps their operating model's rigour to your project's risk profile, ensuring appropriate safeguards are proceduralised.

Decode their commercial model through the team structure

While consultancies rarely publish prices, their proposed team structure reveals their commercial priorities. A team heavy with junior consultants may indicate a low-cost strategy that could compromise quality and senior oversight. Conversely, a top-heavy team might be excellent for strategy but lack the hands-on builders for execution.

The mix of roles proposed is a proxy for their value proposition. A team emphasising data engineers and MLops specialists signals a focus on production durability. A team heavy with data scientists and strategists may be more suited to exploratory work. Ensure the balance matches your primary objective: innovation or operationalisation.

Be wary of models that rely heavily on offshore or nearshore resources without a clear and present onshore leadership team. While distributed teams can be effective, the core accountable leadership must be aligned with your timezone and business context to ensure seamless communication and rapid decision-making when required.

Analyse their commercial model for the presence of a 'Product Owner' role on their side. If this role is billable, it indicates a fixed-scope, delivery-focused engagement. If this role is provided as part of an overhead, it often signals a more collaborative, outcome-based partnership where the consultancy shares the risk and is incentivised to drive continuous business value. The structure of this single role within the contract is a powerful proxy for their entire commercial philosophy.

Decipher the cost structure of their team. A team composed solely of high-day-rate data scientists suggests a commercial model optimised for their revenue, not your outcomes. A blended team with machine learning operators and data engineers, often at different rate cards, demonstrates a commercial alignment with the full AI lifecycle. This structure shows they price for deployment and maintenance efficiency, not just experimental model development, which is crucial for long-term ROI.

Validate their cultural fit and communication cadence

An operating model is not just about roles; it is about rhythms and rituals. Determine their standard communication cadence. Will you receive weekly progress reports, bi-weekly steering committee meetings, and daily stand-ups? The rhythm should provide sufficient oversight without creating unnecessary administrative burden for your team.

Assess their cultural approach to failure and iteration. Do they have blameless post-mortems structured into their process? Is their model designed to learn from mistakes and pivot quickly? This cultural embeddedness within the operating model is crucial for navigating the inherent uncertainties of AI projects successfully.

Finally, evaluate their definition of 'done'. For some vendors, it is the delivery of a model; for others, it is the achievement of a business metric. Their operating model should include clear milestones for handover, documentation, and the point at which your team is fully self-sufficient. This clarity prevents scope creep and ensures a clean conclusion.

Assess cultural fit by proposing a scenario: a key business stakeholder suddenly changes the core objective metric two weeks into a sprint. Observe their reaction. A rigid, technically-focused team might dismiss it as scope creep. A culturally adept team will discuss the business rationale, propose a minimal viable experiment to test the new metric's viability, and outline the impact on the timeline—demonstrating agility and business empathy. This reveals their prioritisation framework in action.

Validate communication cadence by asking them to simulate a post-sprint demo for a non-technical audience. Do they lead with business metrics and visualisations of the model's impact, or do they default to technical jargon about algorithm selection and accuracy scores? Their ability to tailor the message to the audience in a mock setting is the best evidence of their day-to-day communication ethos and their commitment to ensuring your organisation understands the value being delivered.

A five-stage process for evaluating team models

Stage 3: Team Modelling. Create a 'Team Canvas' for the proposed squad. Map their named consultants to roles like 'Data Translator', 'ML Ops Lead', and 'Ethics Champion' based on their CVs and your interviews. Identify glaring gaps—for instance, a team strong on deep learning research but with no dedicated deployment specialist. This visual exercise forces a objective assessment of whether their proposed structure is complete or merely convenient based on who is available on their bench.

Stage 5: Reference Validation. Move beyond standard references. Ask to speak with the client's product owner and a data engineer from their past projects. Prepare specific questions for each: the product owner on business value realisation, the engineer on integration smoothness. This cross-functional reference check provides a triangulated view of their operating model's effectiveness, revealing how well their multi-disciplinary claims hold up under scrutiny from the very people they integrated with.

Prioritise operating model attributes by project type

For a Proof-of-Concept (PoC) project, prioritise attributes that enable speed and learning. The team must have a strong, billable Data Translator role to quickly grasp your business domain and define a valuable use case. Their operating model should emphasise rapid prototyping tools and pre-approved cloud environments to avoid IT procurement delays. The commercial model should be time-and-materials to accommodate pivots, and their communication must include daily stand-ups and weekly demo cycles to maintain momentum and validate assumptions.

For a Full-Scale Deployment project, prioritise risk mitigation and stability. The team structure must include a dedicated, billable ML Ops Engineer responsible for monitoring, scalability, and drift detection. Their operating model requires rigorous version control for data, code, and models, and a clear roll-back strategy. The commercial model should have milestones tied to operational KPIs like inference latency and model stability, not just development completion. Cultural fit must be assessed on their willingness to follow strict change management and compliance protocols.
