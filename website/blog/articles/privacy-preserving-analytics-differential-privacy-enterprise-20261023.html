In short · answer first

Most enterprises should buy a vendor solution for its immediate security, compliance, and cross-platform functionality. Building is only viable for firms with massive, unique data workloads requiring proprietary models and who can sustain a multi-year, multi-million-dollar investment in specialised AI talent and ongoing maintenance.

Key takeaways
  • Initial build costs can exceed £2.5 million before any agent is live
  • Vendor solutions provide immediate compliance with regional data laws
  • Building requires a dedicated team of at least eight senior AI engineers
  • Buying accelerates time-to-value from years to under six months

Defining the Build and Buy Options for Agentic Systems

The 'build' option entails constructing a full-stack Agentic AI system in-house. This includes developing or fine-tuning your own foundational models, building a custom Model Context Protocol (MCP) server framework, and creating the orchestration layer that manages tool use and execution across your chosen instant messaging platforms. It is a ground-up software and infrastructure project demanding deep machine learning expertise.

Conversely, the 'buy' approach involves licensing a pre-built Agentic platform. This vendor-supplied solution typically includes a managed MCP server, pre-configured connectors for major IM platforms like WeCom and Teams, a suite of pre-trained enterprise-ready agents, and the underlying cloud infrastructure. The vendor assumes responsibility for core model updates, security patches, and overall system performance, offering a managed service.

A hybrid 'build-on-buy' strategy is also emerging. Enterprises license a vendor's robust MCP and orchestration platform but then develop proprietary agents tailored to their unique internal tools and data sources. This balances the speed of a purchased foundation with the customisation of building specific high-value agentic workflows for internal operations.

Calculating the True Total Cost of Ownership

The upfront capital expenditure for a build project is substantial. Budget for eight to twelve senior AI engineers, MLOps specialists, and backend developers for a minimum of eighteen months. Factor in compute costs for model training and fine-tuning, which can easily run into hundreds of thousands of pounds. This does not include the indirect costs of project management and infrastructure setup.

With a vendor solution, costs are primarily operational expenditure—a predictable annual or monthly subscription fee. This fee covers all licensing, infrastructure, maintenance, and updates. While the per-seat cost might seem high, it eliminates the massive initial capital outlay and transforms unpredictable engineering costs into a fixed, scalable expense based on actual usage within the organisation.

The most significant TCO differentiator is ongoing maintenance. A built system requires a permanent, expensive team for model retraining, security vulnerability patching, and updating IM platform APIs, which change frequently. A bought solution transfers this continuous burden to the vendor, allowing your internal team to focus on applying AI rather than maintaining it.

Beyond initial licensing, calculate the annual operational overhead for a 'Buy' solution. This includes per-seat license fees, costs for premium support tiers, and mandatory training for administrative staff. For the 'Build' path, factor in the fully burdened cost of your AI/ML engineers, cloud infrastructure for model inference and data processing, and ongoing API consumption fees from LLM providers. A robust TCO model should project these costs over a 3-5 year horizon, applying a realistic annual cost escalation rate (e.g., 3-5%) to account for inflation and increased usage.

Evaluating Your Team's Capabilities and Talent Gap

Building a production-grade Agentic system is not a task for a typical software team. It requires rare and expensive talent: machine learning engineers proficient in fine-tuning large language models, experts in reinforcement learning for agent optimisation, and developers deeply experienced with the MCP specification and building secure, low-latency API servers for tool invocation.

Recruiting this team is a major hurdle. The talent market for these skills is intensely competitive, with long lead times for hiring. You must also consider the opportunity cost; assigning your best engineers to a multi-year platform build means they are not working on core product differentiation or revenue-generating projects for the business.

Choosing to buy sidesteps this talent acquisition crisis. Your existing IT and operations teams can implement and manage the vendor platform with minimal training. Their role shifts from building infrastructure to configuring workflows and managing user adoption, leveraging their deep domain knowledge of your business processes rather than requiring deep AI expertise.

Conduct a skills audit against a required competency matrix. Key roles include a Product Owner to define the agent's conversational flows and business rules, Machine Learning Engineers for fine-tuning and embedding model integration, and DevOps/SREs to containerize the agent and manage its CI/CD pipeline. The most critical talent gap is often a Conversational AI Designer, a specialist who architects the dialogue management and intent classification systems to ensure natural user interactions, a skillset rarely found in standard software teams.

Assessing Time to Value and Strategic Agility

A build project is a long-term strategic bet. The timeline from project kick-off to deploying the first useful agent to a business unit is typically eighteen to twenty-four months. This prolonged period carries significant risk, as the AI competitive landscape and your business needs can evolve dramatically before the project delivers any tangible return.

A commercial vendor solution dramatically compresses this timeline. A proof-of-concept can be running in a matter of weeks, with a full production deployment across an IM platform like DingTalk achievable in three to six months. This rapid time-to-value allows you to respond to competitive threats and new opportunities with much greater agility.

This speed directly impacts your ability to iterate. With a bought platform, you can quickly test agentic workflows in different departments—such as sales support or IT helpdesk—learn from real usage data, and refine your approach. A multi-year build project offers no such flexibility; you are committed to a single technical vision long before you can validate it with users.

Develop a phased rollout plan to achieve incremental value. For example, a 'Buy' solution might deploy a basic FAQ-handling agent to all users within one quarter. In contrast, a 'Build' initiative could aim for a minimal viable agent (MVA) in six months, handling only the top five most frequent ticket types. Measure time to value by tracking the reduction in manual ticket volume and the agent's deflection rate post-deployment, setting clear benchmarks for each phase to validate the investment.

Security, Compliance, and Data Governance Implications

When you build, you retain full control over your data governance and security posture. You can mandate that all data processing, model inference, and tool use occur within your own virtual private cloud or on-premises infrastructure, ensuring compliance with strict data sovereignty laws in markets like mainland China or the European Union. This is a paramount concern for regulated industries.

Reputable vendors, however, invest heavily in security certifications and compliance frameworks that would be cost-prohibitive for a single enterprise to replicate. They offer deployment options that address data residency concerns, such as dedicated regional instances. The key is thorough due diligence to ensure their practices and contractual commitments align with your organisation's risk and regulatory requirements.

The MCP layer itself introduces a security consideration. Agents require access to execute tools and APIs, which creates a new attack surface. A build approach means your team must architect and maintain this security boundary. A vendor should provide a rigorously tested, secure framework for credential management and tool access, with clear audit logs—a significant operational advantage.

For 'Buy', meticulously review the vendor's SOC 2 Type II report, paying specific attention to the data processing addendum (DPA) and their policies for data retention, deletion, and encryption both in transit and at rest. For 'Build', your team must document data lineage from the IM platform to your vector database and establish strict access controls. Implement a regular audit checklist to review all prompts and completions logged for model improvement, ensuring no sensitive customer data is captured without explicit masking and consent.

The Customisation and Vendor Lock-in Trade-off

Building offers ultimate customisation. Your agents can be intricately tailored to proprietary internal software, legacy systems, and highly unique business processes. The MCP server can be designed to work seamlessly with your specific tooling ecosystem, providing a level of integration and efficiency that a general-purpose vendor platform may never achieve, creating a potent competitive moat.

Buying a solution inevitably involves some concession to standardisation. You may need to adapt certain internal processes to fit the vendor's model of agentic workflow. The depth of integration with obscure or custom internal tools may be limited compared to a bespoke build, potentially leaving some edge cases unautomated.

Vendor lock-in is the counterweight to customisation. Adopting a proprietary platform creates switching costs and dependency on the vendor's roadmap, pricing, and continued operation. Mitigate this by prioritising vendors that embrace open standards like MCP, which allows for greater portability of your configurations and agents should you need to change providers in the future.

To mitigate lock-in, insist on contractual guarantees for data portability. This should include the right to export all trained intent models, dialogue trees, and historical interaction logs in a non-proprietary format (e.g., JSON schema). For a built system, avoid tight coupling to a single LLM provider by abstracting API calls behind an internal gateway. This allows for switching models or incorporating open-source alternatives in the future without refactoring the entire agent's core logic, preserving long-term optionality.

A Decision Framework for Enterprise Leaders

Create a weighted scoring matrix with criteria mirroring your strategic priorities (e.g., TCO: 25%, Time-to-Market: 30%, Customization: 20%, Compliance: 25%). Score each option (Build/Buy) from 1-5 on each criterion. The decision rule is clear: if the scores are within a 10% margin, the option with the higher score on the top-weighted criterion wins. If the 'Build' score is higher but your team lacks critical talent, the rule defaults to 'Buy' unless a dedicated budget for hiring and onboarding is pre-approved.

Executing Your Chosen Path: Next Steps

If choosing to buy, immediately form a vendor evaluation committee. Develop a structured proof-of-concept (PoC) script to test all shortlisted platforms against your top ten use cases. For the build path, initiate a two-week discovery sprint. The key artefact from this sprint is a detailed technical specification document outlining the proposed architecture, required APIs, and a refined backlog for the first MVA release. Secure a dedicated cloud sandbox environment for development and testing to accelerate progress from day one.
