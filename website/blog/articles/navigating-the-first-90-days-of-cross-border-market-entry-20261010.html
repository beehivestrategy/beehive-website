Navigating the First 90 Days of Cross-Border Market Entry

In short · answer first

The first 90 days are a discovery sprint, not a full-scale launch. Success hinges on validating your core value proposition with local buyers through direct engagement, establishing a minimal operational footprint for learning, and securing three to five referenceable early clients. This initial period is about de-risking assumptions and building a foundation for scalable growth, not maximising immediate revenue.

Key takeaways
  • Conduct in-market discovery weeks to pressure-test assumptions
  • Secure three to five referenceable pilot clients within 60 days
  • Establish a minimal local entity for compliance and banking
  • Build a 12-month localised roadmap based on initial feedback

Week Zero: Pre-Launch Intelligence Synthesis

Before your team lands, consolidate all existing market intelligence into a single source of truth. This is not about lengthy reports but actionable dashboards. The lead strategist must synthesise data from trade commissions, local legal counsel, and preliminary channel partner discussions. The output is a prioritised list of 50 target accounts and a map of regulatory hurdles specific to your industry vertical.

Assemble a cross-functional landing team of no more than six key individuals. This group must include commercial leadership, a product or service delivery lead, and legal or compliance oversight. Their mandate is exclusively exploratory for the first 30 days. Secure temporary serviced office space and local SIM cards to establish a credible presence from day one, avoiding the perception of a fly-in, fly-out operation.

A critical pre-launch artefact is the Regulatory Compliance Checklist. This document should be owned by your Head of Legal or Compliance Officer and must itemize every local requirement, from data protection (e.g., GDPR in Europe, PDPA in Asia) and tax registration to industry-specific licensing. For example, a fintech entering Brazil must secure authorization from the Central Bank, while a healthtech company in Germany requires approval from the Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices. This checklist transforms abstract legal advice into actionable tasks with clear deadlines and responsible parties, ensuring no critical path item is missed before market activation.

Days 1-30: The In-Market Discovery Sprint

The first month is dedicated to intense, qualitative buyer discovery. The team’s primary key result is to conduct 501x60-minute meetings with potential customers, channel partners, and industry influencers. Focus these conversations on understanding local pain points, budget cycles, and decision committees. Avoid solution pitching; instead, use open-ended questions to validate or invalidate your core value proposition.

Parallel to commercial discovery, the compliance lead must initiate the process of establishing a legal entity. This involves selecting a local corporate structure, engaging a payroll provider, and opening a business bank account, a process known for delays. Begin drafting localised contract templates with counsel to avoid bottlenecks when first pilot deals are ready to be signed.

The Discovery Sprint should be guided by a structured Interview Playbook, managed by the Product Marketing lead. This playbook contains not just questions, but a framework for analysis. It should segment potential interviewees (e.g., 5 potential customers, 3 channel partners, 2 industry analysts) and include a rating system to quantify pain points—e.g., score a problem on a scale of 1-5 for severity and frequency. The output is a synthesized “Problem Prioritization Matrix,” a tangible artefact that moves the team from anecdotal conversations to data-driven insights on which local problems are most acute and commercially viable to solve first.

Concurrently, the lead engineer should conduct a Technical Landscape Audit. This involves hands-on testing of local digital infrastructure: API compatibility with dominant local payment gateways (e.g., Pix in Brazil), mobile network reliability for your app, and performance benchmarking against local CDN providers. The deliverable is a technical risk assessment report that details necessary adaptations, such as integrating with a local SMS provider for two-factor authentication or optimizing data storage for latency. This prevents post-launch technical debt and ensures your product’s core functionality aligns with local technological realities.

Building Your Initial Local Network

Strategic networking is your force multiplier. Prioritise attendance at one major industry conference and two niche meetups within the first 45 days. The goal is not broad visibility but deep connections. Each team member must target five meaningful conversations per event, focusing on potential partners who can provide market credibility and client introductions.

Identify and engage a local non-executive director or advisor. This individual provides invaluable cultural and commercial context, helps navigate unspoken business norms, and opens doors that remain closed to outsiders. Compensate them with a monthly retainer plus success fees for introduced deals that close, aligning their incentives with your market entry goals.

Move beyond generic networking by tasking a business development representative with creating a Strategic Partner Matrix. This tool maps potential allies—local incubators, complementary non-competing SaaS platforms, legal firms—against two axes: their strategic value (access to clients, market credibility) and the required resource investment. A decision rule: prioritize partners who offer a tangible co-marketing plan or a warm introduction to a pilot client within the first 30 days. For example, partnering with a local cloud consultancy can provide immediate access to their installed base, while a membership with the American Chamber of Commerce in your target country can offer high-level regulatory insights and credibility.

Systematize relationship building with a Outreach Cadence Plan. This is a sequenced checklist of touchpoints for each partner category, moving from a initial LinkedIn connection to a virtual coffee meeting, to sharing a relevant piece of your market research. The goal is to provide value first. The Head of Market Entry should track this in a CRM, ensuring no high-potential contact falls through the cracks. Measure success not by meetings held, but by the number of partners who agree to a next-step collaboration meeting, signaling genuine engagement rather than polite interest.

Securing Your First Pilot Clients

By day 45, shift from pure discovery to proposing initial pilot engagements. Target prospects who were most engaged during your discovery process. Structure these first deals as fixed-scope, time-boxed pilots with a significant discount, exchanged for detailed feedback and a case study. The commercial terms must include a clear path to a full-scale agreement.

The legal team must have simplified pilot agreements ready for signature, avoiding complex negotiation cycles. The delivery lead personally oversees the execution of these first pilots, ensuring impeccable service and creating a blueprint for future delivery. A successful pilot is one that generates a referenceable outcome, not just revenue.

To secure pilot clients, develop a Pilot Program Agreement artefact, drafted by Legal. This is distinct from a standard sales contract. It clearly defines the scope of the pilot (e.g., 3-month free usage, 5 named users), the commitments from both sides (your company provides dedicated support, the client provides detailed feedback), and the data rights. Crucially, it includes a mutual success plan with defined milestones and a clear path to a paid contract upon successful completion. This formalizes the relationship, sets expectations, and protects your IP, making larger enterprises more comfortable engaging without an initial financial commitment.

Employ a Pilot Client Scorecard to objectively evaluate candidates. Led by the Sales Lead, this scorecard rates potential pilots on criteria beyond mere willingness: their market influence (e.g., a known brand vs. a startup), strategic fit with your ideal customer profile, and their team’s availability for weekly feedback sessions. A decision rule: only onboard clients who score above a predefined threshold to ensure you gain high-quality, actionable learning. For instance, a highly engaged mid-market company may be a better pilot than a lethargic Fortune 500 brand, as the feedback quality will directly fuel your product iteration.

Operationalising Your Learning

Hold weekly synthesis sessions to document insights across commercial, regulatory, and operational domains. Use a structured template to capture assumptions validated, invalidated, and new hypotheses generated. This living document becomes the foundation for your localised business plan and product roadmap, ensuring decisions are based on observed evidence, not initial assumptions.

Based on this learning, begin localising your core marketing and sales materials. This includes translating key website pages, creating market-specific case studies from your pilots, and adjusting pricing models to match local expectations. Avoid a full rebrand; instead, focus on messaging that resonates with the specific pains uncovered during your discovery interviews.

The process of operationalizing learning requires a central Feedback Log, owned by the Product Manager. This is a living document (e.g., a structured Confluence page or Airtable base) that categorizes all incoming feedback from pilots, network contacts, and discovery interviews. Tags should include: Feature Request, UX Issue, Localization Need, Regulatory Concern, and Pricing Feedback. Each entry must be traced to its source and have a priority status. This transforms scattered notes into a quantifiable backlog, allowing the team to identify patterns—e.g., if 4/5 pilots request the same integration—and make objective decisions on the product roadmap.

Institute a weekly Localization Sync meeting, chaired by the Market Entry Lead. The artefact for this meeting is a Localization Tracker dashboard. It tracks the status of critical adaptations beyond language translation, such as currency and payment processing, date formats, and culturally aligning imagery and content. The decision rule is that no item can be marked ‘complete’ without sign-off from a native in-market team member or trusted pilot client. This ensures adaptations are not just technical but resonate culturally, moving your product from merely available to truly local.

The Day 90 Review and Strategic Pivot

At the 90-day mark, convene a formal review with headquarters leadership. Present a clear go/no-go recommendation based on three criteria: validated product-market fit, a achievable path to profitability, and the ability to scale delivery. Support this with data from pilot outcomes, a refined financial model, and a roster of referenceable clients.

The output of this review is a committed 12-month roadmap and budget for the market. This plan must detail hiring plans for local roles, marketing investment thresholds, and revenue targets. If the data does not support continued investment, have the courage to recommend a pause or pivot, conserving capital for more promising opportunities.

The Day 90 Review must be governed by a formal Agenda and a pre-circulated Data Pack. The Data Pack, prepared by the operations analyst, is the cornerstone. It should contain key metrics dashboards comparing pre-launch assumptions to reality: actual CAC vs. projected, pilot client conversion rates, support ticket volume and themes, and network growth metrics. This moves the review from subjective debate to objective analysis. The agenda should dedicate specific time to reviewing each hypothesis from Week Zero, forcing the team to explicitly state what was validated, invalidated, or is still unknown, creating a clear record of learning.

Based on the data, employ clear Decision Rules for the strategic pivot. For example: if pilot conversion rates are below 20% but feedback indicates a specific feature gap is the primary blocker, the rule is to authorize a development sprint to address it before a full sales launch. If CAC is 300% of projection due to unanticipated marketing channels, the rule is to pivot the go-to-market strategy to focus on lower-cost partner channels. These pre-defined rules, established by leadership before the meeting, reduce ambiguity and emotional decision-making, enabling a swift, confident pivot based on the evidence gathered.

Avoiding Common Day 90 Pitfalls

A frequent failure pattern is mistaking pilot interest for scalable demand. signing five pilots is a success only if they represent a replicable customer segment. Beware of the customised pilot, which requires unsustainable service effort for each new client. The rule of thumb is that your second pilot should require less customisation than the first.

Another critical error is delaying legal entity establishment. The process can take 60-90 days, so initiation in week one is non-negotiable. Operating without a local entity forces you to invoice through offshore accounts, creating tax liabilities and eroding buyer trust. Partnering with a global employment organisation can provide a temporary bridge but is not a long-term solution.

A common pitfall is misinterpreting early signals. To avoid this, the Finance Director should present a analysis separating Vanity Metrics from Validation Metrics. For instance, high website traffic from a launch press release is a vanity metric; the percentage of that traffic that downloads a localized whitepaper or requests a demo is a validation metric. A decision rule: ignore vanity metrics in the Day 90 review. Focus solely on metrics that prove product-market fit and a sustainable commercial model, such as pilot client contract value, qualified lead volume, and partner-generated opportunities, ensuring strategic decisions are based on substantive evidence of traction.

Preparing for the Next 270 Days

With the first 90 days serving as validation, the subsequent quarters focus on scalable growth. Begin recruiting your first local country manager before day 90, ensuring a seamless handover from the initial landing team. This leader will be responsible for executing the approved 12-month plan and building out the full commercial team.

Formalise partnerships with the most effective channel allies identified during the discovery phase. Develop co-branded marketing materials and a partner programme with clear incentives. Simultaneously, invest in a localised digital presence, leveraging insights from initial campaigns to refine targeting and messaging for a broader audience, moving beyond pure founder-led sales.

Preparation for scaling begins with a formalized Handover Plan artefact. This document, created by the Market Entry Lead, outlines the transfer of responsibilities from the launch taskforce to the permanent in-country or regional team. It includes timelines for knowledge transfer sessions, a checklist of key contacts and logins to be handed over, and the status of all ongoing initiatives. For example, the relationship with a key channel partner is formally introduced to the new Channel Manager, and access to the local social media accounts is transferred to the hired Country Marketer, ensuring continuity and preventing operational friction as the team grows.

Finally, draft a Phase Two Investment Proposal. This is a concrete document for executive leadership that outlines the resources required for the next 270 days based on the Day 90 findings. It details the ask: for example, “Funding for a 3-person local sales team based on a CAC payback period of 9 months,” or “Budget for a local brand awareness campaign following the successful validation of our value proposition with pilots.” This proposal translates the first 90 days' learning into a compelling business case for continued investment, securing the budget and resources needed to transition from market entry to growth.
