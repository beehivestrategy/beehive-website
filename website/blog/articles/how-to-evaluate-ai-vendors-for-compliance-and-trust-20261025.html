How to evaluate AI vendors for compliance and trust

In short · answer first

Evaluating AI vendors requires a structured, evidence-based approach focused on proactive compliance and operationalised trust. Move beyond marketing claims to scrutinise their data governance, model accountability, and audit readiness. Your framework must assess both technical safeguards and organisational culture to ensure long-term regulatory alignment.

Key takeaways
  • Demand full access to model cards and audit trails
  • Verify data provenance and consent mechanisms are documented
  • Assess the vendor's incident response and breach notification plan
  • Require evidence of third-party compliance certifications

Establish your compliance non-negotiables first

Before engaging any vendor, your internal team must define the non-negotiable compliance requirements specific to your industry and data types. This list should extend beyond generic GDPR or data residency rules to include sector-specific mandates, such as financial reporting standards or healthcare data handling protocols. This baseline becomes your immutable benchmark for all vendor assessments.

Translate these legal requirements into technical and operational criteria. For instance, if your data cannot leave a specific jurisdiction, this becomes a concrete technical requirement for data processing and storage architecture. Document these criteria in a vendor assessment checklist that will be used uniformly across all evaluations to ensure consistency and objectivity.

Assign ownership for maintaining this checklist within your governance team, mandating quarterly reviews to incorporate new regulations. This living document ensures your organisation adapts to the regulatory landscape proactively, rather than reacting to changes after vendor contracts are signed. It is your first line of defence against compliance debt.

Involve key stakeholders from the outset to define these non-negotiables collaboratively. Your legal counsel should outline contractual red lines, while your data protection officer (DPO) must specify data residency and processing requirements. The head of risk will define acceptable risk thresholds, and IT security will set baseline technical standards. Document these requirements in a formal RFP (Request for Proposal) checklist. This artefact ensures every vendor is measured against the same objective criteria, preventing scope creep and anchoring negotiations in your organisation’s mandated priorities, not the vendor’s marketing claims.

Scrutinise the vendor's data governance framework

A vendor's data governance framework is the bedrock of compliance. Request detailed documentation on their data lineage, provenance, and processing protocols. You must understand precisely how training data was sourced, what consent mechanisms were employed, and how ongoing data inputs are handled. Vague answers here are a significant red flag.

Evaluate their data retention and deletion policies against your own requirements. For example, if your policy mandates user data deletion after 90 days, you must verify the vendor's infrastructure can execute this automatically. Assess their data segregation capabilities to ensure your information is never commingled with other clients' data in a way that creates risk.

Insist on evidence of their data protection impact assessments (DPIAs) for the specific AI services you intend to use. This document should outline identified risks and mitigation strategies. A reputable vendor will have these readily available and tailored to their offering, demonstrating a mature, proactive approach to data governance.

Assess model accountability and audit readiness

True vendor evaluation requires deep scrutiny of model accountability. Demand access to complete model cards and fact sheets for any AI system you will deploy. These documents must detail intended use cases, performance metrics across different subgroups, and known limitations. The absence of such artefacts indicates a lack of maturity.

Probe their audit readiness. Can they produce a full audit trail of a model's decisions upon request? This capability is critical for complying with 'right to explanation' mandates in regulations like GDPR. Test this by requesting a sample audit report for a hypothetical query to assess the clarity and depth of information provided.

Establish who retains liability for model errors or biased outputs. Your contract must clearly define accountability boundaries. For high-risk applications, consider requiring the vendor to undergo a third-party audit by a recognised body before contract signing. This provides an unbiased assessment of their operational controls.

Request evidence of a formal Model Inventory, a living document cataloguing all production models, their intended use cases, and associated risk ratings. For high-stakes applications, insist on access to audit logs from the AI system itself, detailing when the model was retrained, what data was used, and who authorised the deployment. A trustworthy vendor will also provide a completed Responsible AI Impact Assessment (RAIA) for the specific tool you are licensing. This document should transparently outline identified biases, mitigation strategies taken, and residual risks, demonstrating proactive accountability.

Evaluate operational resilience and security postures

Operational resilience is a key component of trust. Examine the vendor's business continuity and disaster recovery plans specifically for their AI services. How do they ensure service continuity if a primary machine learning model fails or is compromised? Their answer should be concrete, tested, and documented.

Conduct a thorough review of their security certifications, but go beyond the certificates themselves. Interview their Chief Information Security Officer or equivalent about how security is integrated into the AI development lifecycle. Ask for specific examples of security reviews conducted for recent model updates to gauge the depth of their practices.

Require a detailed incident response plan that outlines roles, communication protocols, and remediation steps for data breaches or model failures. This plan must align with your own incident management framework to ensure seamless coordination. A tabletop exercise simulating an incident can be a valuable part of the evaluation process.

Analyse contractual terms for compliance alignment

The contract is where compliance promises become enforceable obligations. Scrutinise service level agreements (SLAs) for specific AI performance metrics, not just uptime. For instance, include SLAs for model drift detection and retraining frequency. These ensure the model remains effective and compliant over time, not just at launch.

Pay particular attention to data ownership clauses, intellectual property rights on model improvements, and indemnification terms. Ensure the contract explicitly states that you retain all rights to your data and that any insights generated belong to you. Ambiguity here can lead to significant compliance and legal risks down the line.

Mandate a right-to-audit clause that allows your team or a third party you appoint to conduct compliance audits. Specify the scope, frequency, and process for these audits to avoid disputes. This clause is your ultimate tool for verifying that the vendor's operations match their marketing claims.

Pay meticulous attention to the indemnification clause, ensuring it explicitly covers losses stemming from AI-specific failures, such as algorithmic bias or copyright infringement from generated content. Scrutinise the IP clause to confirm that you retain full ownership of your input data and any unique insights derived from it, while clarifying the license granted for the vendor’s underlying model. Crucially, the liability clause must have uncapped liability for breaches of data protection laws, confidentiality, and IP infringement. Never accept a contract that attempts to aggregate or cap all liability into a single, often low, figure.

Conduct rigorous technical and process validation

Move beyond documentation to hands-on validation. During the trial period, conduct controlled tests using your own data to assess model performance and compliance. Create test cases that probe for bias, inaccuracy, or compliance failures. How the vendor responds to these discovered issues is highly revealing of their culture.

Request a demonstration of their model monitoring and management tools. You need to see the dashboard your team will use to track model performance, data drift, and potential compliance alerts. The tool's usability and depth of information are critical for your ongoing governance responsibilities.

Validate their change management process. How are model updates tested, documented, and communicated? A robust process should include impact assessments, compliance checks, and clear client notifications before deployment. A vendor that allows silent updates creates uncontrollable compliance risk for your organisation.

Go beyond vendor-provided demos by constructing your own test cases using anonymised production data to simulate real-world scenarios. For a customer service chatbot, test its response to sensitive queries about medical or financial information. For a predictive tool, feed it edge cases with missing or anomalous data to assess its default behaviour and robustness. This technical proof-of-concept (PoC) must be run in a sandbox environment that mirrors your own security and compliance settings. The goal is to validate performance and safety in a context that directly reflects your operational reality, not a vendor’s optimised showcase.

Build a continuous evaluation and exit strategy

Vendor evaluation does not end at contract signing. Establish a quarterly business review (QBR) process focused specifically on compliance and trust metrics. These reviews should examine audit logs, incident reports, and performance against the agreed SLAs. This ongoing diligence is essential for maintaining long-term alignment.

Develop a clear exit strategy before you need it. The contract must specify data return and deletion protocols, including how to extract model insights in a usable format. Test these protocols during the relationship to ensure they work effectively, avoiding nasty surprises when transitioning to a new vendor.

Monitor the vendor's regulatory landscape and adapt your requirements accordingly. If new laws come into effect, your QBR process should include a session to reassess compliance and agree on any necessary changes to the service. Treat the vendor as a partner in regulatory adaptation, not just a supplier.

Implement a structured decision framework

Consolidate your findings into a structured decision framework to objectively compare vendors. Score each candidate against weighted criteria such as data governance (30%), model accountability (25%), security (20%), contractual terms (15%), and cultural fit (10%). This quantitative approach reduces bias and justifies the selection to stakeholders.

Involve all relevant functions in the final scoring—including legal, security, data privacy, and business units. Each team will assess the vendor through a different lens, and their collective input provides a holistic view of risk and suitability. Disagreements should be resolved through evidence, not hierarchy.

Document the rationale for your final selection in detail, including the strengths and weaknesses of each candidate. This artefact protects your team if regulatory issues arise later, demonstrating a diligent and thorough selection process. It also provides a benchmark for future vendor evaluations.

Assign quantitative scores to each major criterion (e.g., Data Governance: 25%, Security: 20%, Contract Terms: 15%) to objectively weight their importance against your business context. Establish clear decision rules upfront; for instance, automatically disqualify any vendor that cannot provide a SOC 2 Type II report or that refuses contractual uncapped liability for data breaches. This scoring card, populated by the relevant subject matter experts for each section, transforms a subjective deliberation into a data-driven comparison. It provides an auditable trail justifying the final selection to regulators and internal stakeholders.
