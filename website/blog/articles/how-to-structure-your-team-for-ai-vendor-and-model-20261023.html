How to Structure Your Team for AI Vendor and Model Evaluation

In short · answer first

Successful AI vendor evaluation requires a dedicated, cross-functional team operating under a clear governance model. This team must blend technical, legal, and commercial expertise to assess model performance, data governance, and integration feasibility. Establish a central AI Council for strategic alignment and use a phased evaluation process to de-risk decisions and ensure operational readiness.

Key takeaways
  • Form a core team with a dedicated AI Product Manager and MLOps lead.
  • Establish a lightweight AI Council for strategic alignment and approvals.
  • Implement a three-stage technical evaluation process for each model.
  • Create a vendor scorecard with weighted commercial and technical criteria.

Assemble a Cross-Functional Core Team

Your first action is to form a dedicated core team, moving beyond an ad-hoc committee. This group must own the end-to-end evaluation process. Mandate roles for an AI Product Manager, a Technical Lead specialising in foundation models, a dedicated MLOps Engineer, and a Legal Counsel with data licensing expertise. This core team operates as a single point of accountability, ensuring continuity and deep institutional knowledge throughout the vendor selection lifecycle.

The AI Product Manager is the crucial linchpin, translating business needs into technical requirements and vice versa. This role differs from a standard product manager; they must deeply understand model capabilities, limitations, and the operational cadence of model updates. They are responsible for the evaluation backlog, prioritising tests based on business impact, and maintaining the vendor scorecard. This role ensures the process remains aligned to commercial outcomes, not just technical benchmarks.

The Technical Lead and MLOps Engineer form the execution engine. The lead architect designs the evaluation framework, including stress tests and red-teaming scenarios. The MLOps engineer builds the scaffolding for rapid model testing, ensuring reproducible environments and managing the infrastructure cost of evaluations. Their collaboration is vital for moving a model from a demo environment to a staged production setting for final validation.

A critical artefact for this core team is a RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) matrix, clearly defining involvement for each evaluation phase. For instance, the Data Scientist is Responsible for running bias audits, the Legal Counsel is Consulted on data privacy implications, and the Head of the business unit is Accountable for the final vendor selection. This prevents ambiguity and ensures the right stakeholder is driving each critical decision, streamlining the process and avoiding bottlenecks where input is missing or responsibilities are contested.

Establish a Lightweight AI Governance Council

The core team requires strategic oversight and air cover, provided by a lightweight AI Council. This body should include the Head of Data, the CTO or CIO, the Chief Risk Officer, and a senior business sponsor. The council does not run the evaluations but meets fortnightly to provide strategic direction, approve major budget allocations, and ratify the core team's final vendor recommendations.

Define a clear decision-rights framework for the council to prevent bottlenecks. The council owns the high-level vendor strategy and budget. The core team owns the execution of evaluations and the technical recommendation. For example, the council approves the evaluation budget for a shortlisted vendor, while the core team decides which specific model sizes to test based on their technical findings.

Utilise a RACI matrix for all major evaluation milestones to formalise accountability. This prevents ambiguity during critical steps like contract negotiation or security review. The council is informed on progress, consulted on strategic pivots, and approves final decisions. This structure ensures business leaders are engaged without micromanaging the technical deep dive.

The council's primary tool for efficient decision-making should be a pre-approved decision rubric. This document outlines clear thresholds for automatic approval, review, or rejection based on the core team's scorecard. For example, any vendor with a security score below 80% is automatically rejected, while a model with a bias metric within a predefined acceptable range can be approved without a full council review. This empowers the core team to proceed on clear-cut cases and reserves valuable council time for nuanced, high-stakes, or borderline evaluations that truly require executive judgement.

Implement a Phased Technical Evaluation Process

Adopt a three-stage technical evaluation process to systematically de-risk model selection. Stage One is a lightweight screening using vendor-provided benchmarks and a small set of internal canonical tasks. This stage quickly filters out obviously unsuitable candidates, focusing resources on the most promising two or three vendors. The output is a go/no-go decision for a full technical deep dive.

Stage Two is the comprehensive technical deep dive. The core team executes a rigorous battery of tests on the vendor's dedicated instance. This includes quantitative testing on a held-out evaluation set of several thousand examples, qualitative red-teaming for harmful outputs, and performance benchmarking under expected production load. This stage produces a detailed technical report for the AI Council.

Stage Three is a limited production pilot for the finalist. Deploy the model in a isolated staging environment to validate performance on real user traffic and assess full integration costs. This final validation step uncovers operational realities, such as latency issues or unanticipated MLOps overhead, before a full enterprise-wide commitment is made. The pilot's success metrics form the basis for the final business case.

Phase 1 (Proof-of-Concept) must conclude with a formal go/no-go gate review. The entry ticket for this review is a completed checklist signed by all core team leads. This checklist includes artefacts like a preliminary model performance report on a held-out test set, a completed vendor security assessment, and a high-level architecture diagram for integration. A decision rule, such as requiring a minimum 90% accuracy benchmark and no critical security vulnerabilities, determines if the evaluation progresses to the more resource-intensive Pilot phase, ensuring effort is not wasted on unsuitable candidates.

Develop a Standardised Vendor Scorecard

Create a weighted vendor scorecard to objectify comparisons and mitigate individual bias. This artefact should contain five primary categories: Model Performance, Data Security & Governance, Commercial Terms, Integration & Operations, and Vendor Strength. Assign a percentage weight to each category based on your organisation's priorities, for example, giving Model Performance a 40% weighting if accuracy is the paramount concern.

Within each category, define specific, measurable criteria. Under Model Performance, include metrics like accuracy on your key tasks, inference latency, and output consistency. Under Commercial Terms, score criteria like pricing model predictability, termination rights, and audit clauses. This granularity transforms subjective opinions into quantifiable scores, allowing for a data-driven shortlist and final selection.

The scorecard is a living document maintained by the AI Product Manager. Scores are populated collaboratively by the core team member responsible for each domain. The final score provides a clear, auditable rationale for the recommendation presented to the AI Council. It also serves as a valuable archive for future re-evaluations, showing how vendor landscapes evolve.

Beyond technical specs, the scorecard must rigorously assess operational and commercial viability. Dedicate a section to Vendor Stability & Support, scored on factors like years in business, financial health, number of dedicated support engineers, and average response time for critical tickets. Another section, Implementation & Total Cost of Ownership (TCO), should score the clarity of their implementation plan, required internal effort, and a full cost breakdown including not just licensing but also estimated costs for data processing, cloud infrastructure, and ongoing maintenance over a 3-5 year horizon.

Define Clear Model Readiness Levels

Implement a Model Readiness Level framework, adapted from technology readiness levels, to communicate evaluation progress. Define levels one through five, for example from 'Initial Screening' to 'Production Certified'. This system provides a common language for the core team and the AI Council to discuss risk and progress without requiring deep technical briefings at every update.

Each readiness level should have explicit exit criteria. A model advances to Level Three, 'Technical Validation Complete', only after passing all quantitative benchmarks and red-teaming scenarios. Advancement to Level Four, 'Pilot Validation', requires a signed pilot agreement and confirmed staging environment resources. These clear gates prevent a model from progressing based on hype or vendor pressure alone.

Publish the current readiness level of all evaluated models on an internal dashboard visible to the AI Council and key stakeholders. This creates transparency and manages expectations. It clearly shows which models are still under evaluation, which are pilot candidates, and which are fully approved for broader deployment, streamlining demand from other business units.

Each MRL level requires specific, signed-off artefacts for progression. To move from MRL 2 (Lab Validated) to MRL 3 (Pilot Ready), the team must produce a model card detailing performance characteristics, a fairness and bias audit report, and an infrastructure impact assessment. Advancement to MRL 4 (Production Ready) necessitates a successful pilot report with measured business outcomes, a finalized API integration design document, and a signed service level agreement (SLA) from the vendor. These concrete deliverables create clear, quality gates and prevent premature operational handover.

Build a Repeatable Contract Negotiation Playbook

Develop a standard playbook for negotiating AI vendor contracts, focusing on the unique clauses these agreements require. Centralise this playbook with your Legal Counsel on the core team. It should contain fallback positions and preferred language for critical issues like data processing agreements, intellectual property indemnification, and service level agreements for uptime and latency.

The playbook must address model-specific risks. Key clauses include guarantees on model version availability, stipulations for handling vendor model updates that could break your application, and clear terms for data usage for model improvement. Negotiating these points early prevents costly operational disruptions or legal challenges after deployment.

Mandate that Legal Counsel conducts a preliminary review of a vendor's standard agreement during Stage One. This identifies potential deal-breakers before significant evaluation resources are spent. This early legal engagement ensures the commercial and legal feasibility of a vendor is assessed in parallel with their technical capabilities, not as an afterthought.

The playbook must include pre-vetted fallback clauses for critical negotiation points. For instance, if a vendor refuses to accept liability for breaches originating from their model, the playbook instructs the legal team to immediately counter with a predefined alternative clause that limits liability to a specific percentage of the annual contract value. This preparation prevents negotiators from being caught off-guard and allows them to respond confidently with legally-approved alternatives, significantly speeding up the negotiation cycle and improving outcomes.

Another key element is a cost benchmarking database within the playbook. This should contain anonymized data on previously negotiated rates for similar AI services, broken down by compute cost per inference, user license fees, and support costs. Armed with this data, the procurement team can confidently push back on inflated pricing, citing concrete market comparisons. This moves the discussion from subjective haggling to an objective, data-driven negotiation on value, ensuring the company secures a fair and competitive price.

Plan for Continuous Model Re-evaluation

The vendor selection is not a one-time event but the start of a continuous relationship. Assign your MLOps lead the ongoing responsibility of monitoring model performance and vendor service levels. Establish automated monitoring for performance drift against your key metrics and track the vendor's release notes for upcoming model changes that may impact your operations.

Schedule formal quarterly business reviews with your incumbent vendors. These reviews should assess performance against the agreed scorecard criteria, discuss roadmap alignment, and address any operational issues. This structured engagement ensures you derive continued value and are aware of upcoming changes, such as price increases or model deprecations.

Formalise an annual light-touch re-evaluation process. The core team should briefly reassess the competitive landscape by running Stage One evaluations on one or two emerging challengers. This practice ensures your organisation remains aware of market innovations and provides leverage during annual contract renewals, ensuring you continue to receive the best possible service and value.

Establish an automated monitoring dashboard as the central artefact for continuous evaluation. This dashboard should track a curated set of metrics against predefined thresholds, including: model performance (e.g., accuracy drift), business impact (e.g., change in conversion rate), and operational health (e.g., latency, error rates). The governance council must define the decision rules triggered by these metrics, such as 'if accuracy drift exceeds 5% for three consecutive days, automatically generate a ticket for the data science team to investigate and retrain.'

The re-evaluation process itself must be scheduled and structured. Beyond ad-hoc triggers, mandate a lightweight quarterly business review (QBR) for each production model. This review, led by the core team, assesses the model's continued business value against newer alternatives in the market. The output is a simple recommendation: continue as-is, invest in optimization, or initiate a replacement evaluation. This institutionalizes a rhythm for ensuring your AI capabilities never become stale and remain aligned with evolving strategic goals.

Transition from Evaluation to Operational Handover

A successful evaluation concludes with a clean handover to the operational engineering teams. The core team must produce a final model package for handover. This artefact includes the final scorecard, the model card detailing performance characteristics, the validated infrastructure code, and the agreed service level objectives.

The handover process includes two weeks of embedded support from the core team's MLOps engineer. They work alongside the receiving platform team to ensure a smooth transition, knowledge transfer, and understanding of operational nuances. This prevents the model from becoming a 'black box' that only the evaluation team can support, building broad operational resilience.

Finally, the AI Product Manager should conduct a retrospective on the evaluation process itself. Capture lessons learned on what worked well in the operating model and what could be improved. Update the team's playbooks and scorecards accordingly. This continuous improvement of your evaluation capability will make subsequent vendor assessments more efficient and effective.

The handover process is formalized through a mandatory Operational Readiness Checklist. This document, co-signed by the evaluation core team and the receiving MLOps/infrastructure team, verifies that all prerequisites are met. Key items include: full documentation transferred to the knowledge base, monitoring and alerting configured in the central dashboard, a runbook for incident response completed, and a knowledge transfer session conducted. No handover is authorized without this checklist's completion, preventing operational teams from inheriting poorly documented or supported models.

Finally, define the precise moment of handover as the closure of a dedicated 'vendor evaluation' ticket in the project tracking system and the simultaneous opening of a new, permanent 'model maintenance' ticket assigned to the operational team. This ticket includes all relevant artefacts from the evaluation: the final scorecard, the signed contract SLA, and the model card. This ritual clearly demarcates the end of the project phase and the beginning of the model's lifecycle as a monitored production asset, ensuring long-term accountability.
