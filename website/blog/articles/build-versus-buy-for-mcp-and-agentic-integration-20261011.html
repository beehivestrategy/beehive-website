Build versus Buy for MCP and Agentic Integration

In short · answer first

The build versus buy decision hinges on strategic differentiation versus operational efficiency. Building offers customisation for unique workflows but demands immense, sustained investment in talent and infrastructure. Buying accelerates time-to-value and provides vendor-managed scalability, but may require adapting internal processes to the platform's capabilities. Most enterprises find a hybrid approach optimal.

Key takeaways
  • Building requires a dedicated team of at least eight specialists for initial development
  • Total cost of ownership for a built solution often exceeds initial projections by 40-60%
  • Buying accelerates deployment, with initial agent workflows live within 90 days
  • A hybrid strategy allows for custom agent design on a managed platform

Defining the Strategic Imperative

Your initial strategic imperative dictates the entire programme's trajectory. Is the goal to achieve a defensible competitive moat through proprietary agent logic deeply embedded in unique business processes? Or is the primary objective enterprise-wide efficiency gains and rapid user adoption across familiar collaboration tools? The answer determines whether you invest in building a core capability or buying a scalable service.

A build strategy is justified when your agents must execute highly specialised, business-critical workflows that constitute a primary revenue stream or operational advantage. For instance, an investment bank requiring real-time, compliance-governed trade execution agents within Bloomberg Chat necessitates a custom build. The cost is high, but the differentiation is clear and defensible.

Conversely, a buy strategy suits organisations prioritising standardisation, speed, and broad enablement. If the goal is to deploy customer service triage agents across WeCom, DingTalk, and WhatsApp simultaneously, a vendor platform managing the underlying models and integrations is far more efficient. This approach optimises for operational scale, not unique capability.

The True Cost of Building In-House

The apparent cost of building is the initial team assembly. You will require agentic framework engineers, MCP server specialists, LLM optimisation experts, and devops engineers for orchestration. This core team of eight to twelve senior engineers represents a significant annual investment before any infrastructure or model costs are considered. This team must be retained for the system's entire lifecycle.

Beyond personnel, infrastructure costs are substantial and ongoing. You must budget for GPU clusters or cloud compute credits for model inference, vector database licensing, and the continuous integration/deployment pipeline. Each integrated platform—like Feishu or Teams—requires dedicated development and maintenance for its API, which can change without notice, demanding constant vigilance and adaptation.

The most underestimated cost is opportunity cost. The eighteen to twenty-four months spent building a robust platform is time not spent deploying value-generating agents. Competitors leveraging bought solutions will have agents in production, learning and iterating, while your team is still architecting the foundational infrastructure. This lead time can be decisive in fast-moving markets.

The Operational Realities of a Buy Decision

Choosing a commercial MCP platform shifts the burden of core infrastructure maintenance and model currency to the vendor. Your internal team transitions from builders to configurators and composers. They focus on designing effective agent workflows, crafting precise prompts, and integrating with internal data sources, rather than managing Kubernetes clusters or model fine-tuning pipelines. This accelerates initial deployment dramatically.

However, buying introduces a dependency on the vendor's roadmap and architectural choices. Your ability to innovate may be constrained by the platform's supported model providers, its rate limits, or its approach to contentious issues like grounding and hallucination mitigation. You must evaluate whether the platform's vision for agentic work aligns with your long-term strategic needs beyond the first use cases.

Integration into enterprise governance and security frameworks remains your responsibility. The commercial platform must be vetted for data residency, compliance with regional regulations like China's data laws, and adherence to internal audit trails. While the vendor provides tools, ensuring everything meets corporate standards requires significant security and legal review cycles.

A Hybrid Approach: Orchestrating the Best of Both

For most enterprises, a purist build or buy approach is suboptimal. A hybrid strategy leverages a commercial MCP platform for its managed infrastructure, multi-IM connectivity, and core agent orchestration while allowing for custom development where true differentiation exists. This is the architecture of choice for balancing speed with strategic control.

In this model, you use the bought platform as the router and workflow engine. It handles the connections to WeCom, DingTalk, and Teams, manages context windows, and calls upon standard tools. For unique, high-value capabilities—like a proprietary risk modelling tool or a custom CRM action—you develop bespoke MCP servers that plug into the platform as first-class citizens.

This approach future-proofs your investment. As the commercial platform evolves, your common agents benefit from upgrades automatically. Your custom servers, which embody your unique IP, can be maintained and improved independently. This creates a clear separation of concerns: vendor-managed scale for common tasks, and your team-owned excellence for proprietary ones.

The Team Structures for Each Path

Pursuing a build path mandates assembling a dedicated product team structure. This requires a product manager to define the roadmap, a technical lead for architecture, and engineers specialised in Python, LLM optimisation, and API design. This team operates as an internal startup, responsible for the platform's entire lifecycle, from user research to deployment and support.

Opting to buy demands a different team composition. You need a solution architect to evaluate vendors, a programme manager to oversee rollout, and citizen developers or business technologists from operational units. These individuals design the agent workflows because they possess the domain knowledge. Central IT provides governance and ensures security compliance across these distributed efforts.

The hybrid model necessitates a centre of excellence (CoE) team. This small group of platform engineers manages the relationship with the commercial vendor, establishes development standards for custom MCP servers, and provides tools and training to business units. They enable scale without losing control, acting as force multipliers for the entire organisation.

Evaluating Vendors for a Multi-IM Strategy

Vendor evaluation must be ruthlessly practical. Create a scored matrix assessing critical capabilities. Primary criteria must include native support for your required IM platforms—not just API access, but deep, feature-complete integration with WeCom's private APIs or DingTalk's organisational context. Without this, deployment becomes an internal development project, negating the buy advantage.

Equally vital is the vendor's approach to model agility. The platform should allow you to route different agent tasks to different LLMs (e.g., GPT-4 for reasoning, Claude for summarisation, a local model for sensitive data) and switch providers as technology evolves. Lock-in to a single model provider is a significant long-term risk that undermines resilience and cost management.

Scrutinise the vendor's security and compliance posture. Demand detailed documentation on data encryption in transit and at rest, audit trails for agent actions, and compliance certifications relevant to your regions. For Hong Kong deployments, this often means understanding their data hosting locations and protocols for handling cross-border data flow, a non-negotiable for financial services firms.

A Practical Roadmap for Implementation

Initiate a time-boxed proof of concept (PoC) regardless of your chosen path. For a build evaluation, the PoC goal is to successfully execute one complex agent workflow across two IM platforms to stress-test the architecture. For a buy evaluation, the goal is to deploy a simple but real workflow on the vendor's platform within thirty days to assess usability.

Based on the PoC, make your final build, buy, or hybrid decision. Socialise the findings with key stakeholders, highlighting the proven capabilities, identified risks, and resource requirements. This evidence-based decision prevents the choice from being derailed by individual preferences or outdated assumptions about what the organisation can realistically achieve and maintain.

Execute a phased rollout. Begin with a low-risk, high-volume use case like IT helpdesk triage on Teams or HR policy queries on Feishu. This builds organisational confidence and generates valuable usage data. Gradually progress to more complex and business-critical workflows, continuously refining your governance, training, and support models based on real-world experience.

Measuring Success and Iterating

Define success metrics before deployment. For efficiency-focused agents, track mean time to resolution (MTTR) for user queries or reduction in ticket volume. For complex agents aimed at revenue or innovation, measure conversion rates or the time saved by human experts. Establish a baseline before go-live to quantify the agent's actual impact, not just its activity.

Monitor operational metrics rigorously. Track latency per agent response, success rates of tool calls, and hallucination incidence. These technical indicators are leading indicators of user satisfaction and business value. A poorly performing agent erodes trust rapidly. Implement a feedback loop within each IM channel, allowing users to report errors or provide praise easily.

Plan for continuous iteration. The most successful programmes treat agent deployment as the start of a cycle, not the finish. Use the collected metrics and user feedback to retool prompts, add new data sources, or even retire underperforming agents. This agile approach ensures your investment continues to deliver increasing value and adapts to changing business conditions.
