Enterprise AI Q3 Review: Preparing for a Strong Q4 Finish

BK
Beehive Strategy · September 1, 2025 · 5 min read

The Q3 AI review should be a kill-and-fund exercise. The direct answer for enterprises closing Q3 2025: score every active AI initiative on evidence of value and path to production, kill the pilots that cannot show a sponsor, a baseline, or a route to production — Gartner predicts 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, and your portfolio should make that cut deliberately rather than by accident — and reallocate the freed budget to the two or three initiatives with measured impact. Then enter Q4 with a governed portfolio, a named owner per initiative, and a fiscal year 2026 proposal grounded in results rather than promises.

Key Insight: McKinsey's State of AI research found 65% of organizations were regularly using generative AI in 2024, and Gartner forecasts more than 80% of enterprises will have used GenAI APIs or deployed GenAI-enabled applications by 2026. Adoption is no longer the differentiator — portfolio discipline is: which initiatives reach production, which get killed on evidence, and which get the budget for Q4 and FY26.

Strategic Context and Market Dynamics

September is when the year's AI investments meet the annual plan, and the market context makes the review more consequential than usual. The compliance landscape has matured through 2025 — regulators across jurisdictions have moved from guidance toward enforcement patterns, and organizations with governed AI estates are finding audits far easier than those with ad hoc deployments. The spending picture is equally demanding: with AI spend growing at a double-digit rate on top of multi-trillion-dollar IT budgets, the CFO's question is no longer "should we invest in AI" but "which of our AI investments are earning their keep." McKinsey's 65% adoption figure says the deployment wave has happened; the Q3 review is where enterprises separate what that wave produced from what it merely consumed.

The second dynamic is the end-of-year funding cycle. Decisions made in September and October determine the FY26 budget proposal, and initiatives that enter Q4 without a sponsor, a baseline, or a production plan are not going to acquire those things in the holiday quarter — they are going to absorb budget that could fund the initiatives with evidence. The gap between leaders and laggards in AI adoption is widening, and the mechanism is visible in exactly this review: leaders cut decisively on evidence and redeploy, while laggards keep everything alive and fund nothing properly.

Key Decision Points for Enterprise Leaders

Three decisions dominate the Q3 review. The first is scale versus kill for every pilot: each initiative gets scored on evidence of value (has it moved a business metric?) and path to production (does it have data access, a sponsor, a governance model, and a date?). The honest outcome of such a scorecard is that some pilots die — that is the point — and the budget they were consuming flows to the survivors. The second decision is build versus buy versus managed service for the initiatives that survive: with integration and data engineering consuming 40–60% of typical AI project budgets, enterprises are increasingly choosing a managed conversational layer that turns that fixed cost into a subscription and shows real answers within weeks rather than quarters.

The third decision is the platform question: does the organization fund the shared layer — connectors, semantic definitions, governance tooling — that makes every use case cheaper, or does it keep funding point solutions that each rebuild the integration? The enterprises that will be able to show strong Q4 results are the ones that answered this question in favor of the platform earlier in the year; the Q3 review is the last chance to redirect before FY26 planning locks in. Every dollar spent on a reusable connector is a dollar that does not need to be spent on the next pilot's bespoke integration.

Which AI Initiatives Should You Cut Before Q4?

Cut anything that fails one of these tests, and be willing to defend the cut in the FY26 narrative as portfolio discipline, not failure:

  • No business sponsor — an initiative whose value no business unit will own and measure is a hobby, not a program; it cannot report ROI because no one is accountable for it.
  • No baseline or metric — if the "before" state was never measured, the initiative can never demonstrate a delta, which means it can never be defended in a budget review.
  • Stalled in proof of concept — pilots that have run past twelve months without a production date are exactly the projects behind Gartner's 30% abandonment prediction; time-box the rest and kill the ones that missed it.
  • No governed data access — an initiative that cannot show access controls, audit logging, and lineage is a compliance exposure, and 2025's enforcement trends make that exposure a real cost.
  • Duplicate capability — two initiatives solving the same problem on different integrations should merge into one, with the loser's budget redeployed to the shared platform layer.

The counterintuitive discipline is to also cut initiatives that are "working fine" but small: a pilot that delivers value only to one team, on bespoke infrastructure, at pilot scale, is a candidate for consolidation into the platform rather than a candidate for independent scale. Q4 is when the platform should be getting the investment, because FY26 use cases will run on whatever was built in Q4.

Organizational Readiness Assessment

Use the review to assess readiness for the Q4 push and the FY26 plan. Four questions capture the state. First, data: are the high-value data sources connected, documented, and governed, or is the first quarter of 2026 going to be consumed by integration work that could start now? Second, sponsorship: does every surviving initiative have a business owner with a named metric and a quarterly review, or is the portfolio still run by IT on behalf of nobody in particular? Third, governance: is there a defined process for access control, audit, and answer evaluation, or would a compliance review of the AI estate produce surprises — and with regulatory enforcement maturing, that review is coming? Fourth, adoption: do the business teams that will use the AI in Q4 know what to ask, and is there a change-management plan, or does the organization expect adoption to happen by itself?

The readiness assessment is also the FY26 budgeting input. McKinsey's transformation research consistently finds that sponsorship, ownership, and governance alignment — not technology choice or budget size — predict outcomes, and organizations with C-suite champions and cross-functional governance report materially faster time-to-value than those run from IT alone. If the Q3 review reveals weak sponsorship, the fix is a people decision, not a technology decision, and it needs to happen before the FY26 plan is written.

Measuring Success and ROI

The Q3 review needs a measurement framework with three tiers, and the survivors should be reporting against all of them before Q4 begins. Operational metrics track efficiency — time-to-answer, automation rates, cost per transaction or per query. Business metrics translate efficiency into outcomes — cost saved, margin recovered, cycle time reduced, revenue protected. Strategic metrics capture capability — pilot-to-production rate, time-to-value for new use cases, and the declining cost of each successive deployment as the platform layer compounds. An initiative reporting only operational metrics is an IT project; one reporting only strategic ambition is a vision; the funded portfolio reports all three.

Baselines are the enforcement mechanism. Every surviving initiative should have its "before" captured now, in Q3, so that Q4 and FY26 reporting has a delta to show. The discipline of re-measuring monthly — rather than quarterly — is what makes the numbers defensible in a year-end review, and it is what lets the organization kill a failing initiative in its second month of Q4 instead of discovering the failure in January. Measurement is not a reporting exercise; it is the steering mechanism for the Q4 budget and the evidence base for the FY26 ask.

Actionable Recommendations for H2 2025

Close the year with five concrete moves. First, run the scorecard now — before the end of September — so the kills are deliberate, documented, and framed as portfolio discipline in the FY26 narrative. Second, reallocate the freed budget to the shared platform layer: connectors, semantic definitions, and governance tooling are the fixed costs that make every 2026 use case cheaper, and they are the line items most at risk in budget squeezes. Third, pick one high-value conversational use case — executive KPI monitoring, operational exceptions, or customer-service deflection — and commit to real answers in production during Q4; a measured win before year-end changes the tone of every FY26 funding conversation. Fourth, set the FY26 rules now: quarterly reviews, kill criteria, baseline requirements, and a named sponsor for every funded line item. Fifth, if the platform is not yet in place, make the Q4 decision to buy it as a managed service — a governed conversational BI layer can be live in about two weeks, which means it can be producing measured answers before the year closes.

The enterprises that will lead in 2026 are the ones treating the Q3 review as the moment of discipline: kill on evidence, fund the platform, measure the survivors, and enter Q4 with a portfolio that can be defended line by line. The decisions of the next ninety days determine not just the Q4 close but the competitive position for the fiscal year ahead.

Recent research underscores the magnitude of this transformation. A McKinsey survey from mid-2025 reveals that 72% of enterprises have at least one AI pilot in production, yet only 23% have scaled beyond a single department. Perhaps more significantly, The average enterprise AI budget has increased by 34% year-over-year, with the largest allocation shift going toward ROI measurement and operationalization. These findings suggest that we are at a critical juncture where the organizations that get enterprise strategy right will create lasting competitive advantages, while those that hesitate risk being permanently displaced. The stakes for talent have never been higher.
