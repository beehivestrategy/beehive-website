How to evaluate AI vendors for regulatory compliance and trust

In short · answer first

Evaluating AI vendors requires a structured process that moves beyond technical features to scrutinise operational governance, compliance documentation, and ethical safeguards. Begin by mapping vendor capabilities directly to your specific regulatory obligations. Then, conduct rigorous technical and contractual audits to ensure their systems are transparent, accountable, and align with your organisation's risk tolerance and trust requirements.

Key takeaways
  • Map vendor capabilities directly to your regulatory obligations
  • Demand comprehensive documentation of model provenance and data lineage
  • Verify independent audit trails for all compliance claims
  • Establish clear contractual terms for liability and incident response

Establish your regulatory baseline first

Before engaging any vendor, your compliance team must first codify the exact regulatory requirements your organisation faces. This is not a generic list but a specific mapping of obligations from frameworks like the EU AI Act, local data protection laws, and sector-specific rules. Create a master register that details each rule, its jurisdictional applicability, and the potential penalties for non-compliance.

This register becomes your evaluation scorecard. For each requirement, define the evidence a vendor must provide to demonstrate adherence. For instance, a high-risk AI system mandate for fundamental rights impact assessments requires a vendor to supply their assessment methodology and sample reports. This pre-work ensures every vendor conversation is focused and evidence-based from the outset.

Assign ownership for maintaining this register, typically within your chief compliance officer’s remit. It must be a living document, updated quarterly to reflect new guidance and enforcement actions. This proactive stance prevents your organisation from being caught off-guard by regulatory shifts and ensures your vendor evaluations remain current and rigorous.

To build this baseline, convene a cross-functional team including Legal, Compliance, Data Privacy, and Risk Management officers. Their first task is to map all applicable regulations—like GDPR's Article 22 for automated decision-making or sector-specific rules like NYDFS Cybersecurity—to your intended AI use case. Create a simple compliance matrix, a spreadsheet with columns for Regulation, Relevant Clause, Impact Level (High/Medium/Low), and Responsible Internal Owner. This artefact transforms abstract legal text into a concrete, actionable checklist for vendor evaluation, ensuring no critical obligation is overlooked during initial vendor screenings.

Scrutinise the vendor's operational governance

A vendor’s sales pitch often highlights product features, but your due diligence must probe their operational backbone. Request their organisational chart specifically identifying roles responsible for model governance, data ethics, and compliance assurance. The absence of dedicated, senior-level positions for these functions is a significant red flag requiring further investigation.

Evaluate the concrete processes they have implemented. Demand evidence of their model change management protocol, data lineage tracking from ingestion to inference, and their incident response playbook for model failure or bias detection. These are not theoretical documents but operational artefacts that should show clear workflows, approval gates, and audit trails.

Assess the culture by requesting minutes from their AI ethics board meetings or summaries of employee training completion rates. A vendor’s commitment to responsible AI is demonstrated through repeated investment and documented deliberation, not marketing slogans. This cultural due diligence is critical for long-term partnership stability.

Request the vendor's RACI chart for their AI governance processes. Identify who is Accountable for model approval and who is Responsible for daily monitoring. Scrutinise the reporting structure: does the Chief AI Ethics Officer report directly to the board or is it a diluted function within engineering? A key decision rule is to reject vendors where compliance roles lack independence and authority. Furthermore, examine their change management protocol. A robust vendor will have a formalised process, including a Change Advisory Board that must approve any major model update, ensuring stability and continuous compliance.

Demand comprehensive documentation and provenance

For any regulated use case, insist on a full model card and detailed fact sheet. This documentation must transparently disclose training data sources, methodologies, known limitations, and performance metrics across relevant demographic segments. Reject vague statements; require precise figures and definitions for all metrics reported.

Verify data provenance rigorously. The vendor must provide a complete audit trail for their training data, demonstrating clear copyright and licensing for all materials and documenting all preprocessing steps, including labelling methodologies and bias mitigation techniques. This is non-negotiable for meeting transparency obligations under modern AI regulations.

Finally, require access to detailed API logs and usage metadata as a condition of procurement. Your ability to monitor the model’s performance in production and to conduct your own post-deployment audits is essential for compliance. Contractually mandate the vendor’s provision of this data in a standardised, machine-readable format.

Beyond high-level summaries, demand the Data Card and Model Card for your specific instance. The Data Card should detail the provenance, composition, and preprocessing of the training datasets, including any synthetic data used. Look for specific artefacts like a bill of materials (BOM) that lists every data source and its licence. For the model, the card must explicitly state its limitations, known biases uncovered during testing, and the demographic makeup of its fairness testing cohorts. This granular documentation is your primary evidence for demonstrating due diligence to regulators.

Verify third-party audits and certifications

Vendor self-assessments are insufficient for a high-assurance environment. Prioritise vendors that undergo regular, independent third-party audits against recognised frameworks like ISO 42001 or the NIST AI RMF. Scrutinise the audit reports themselves, not just the certification badges, paying close attention to the scope, findings, and corrective action plans.

Be wary of audits that are too narrow in scope. An audit of data centres for SOC 2 compliance does not equate to an audit of AI system fairness. The right audit should specifically assess the AI lifecycle stages relevant to your use case, from data collection and model training to deployment and monitoring.

Understand the auditor’s qualifications and expertise. The field of AI auditing is still emerging, and not all audit firms possess the technical depth required. Prefer vendors that use auditors with a publicly stated specialism in AI and machine learning systems, as this adds a layer of credibility to their findings.

Do not accept a certificate at face value; request the full audit report's executive summary and findings appendix. Specifically, check for detailed notations and any remediated minor non-conformities, which demonstrate a mature process. For a SOC 2 Type II report, go directly to the section on logical access controls and user lifecycle management to verify how the vendor enforces the principle of least privilege. A worked example: if an audit report shows past findings around inadequate logging, verify the vendor's evidence that these gaps were closed within 90 days, proving responsive governance.

Evaluate technical architecture for control and transparency

The vendor’s technical architecture must provide your team with necessary controls. For sensitive applications, determine if the offering supports fully air-gapped or private cloud deployment, ensuring data never leaves your environment. If a SaaS model is proposed, encrypt data both in transit and at rest using your organisation’s managed keys.

Probe the observability features built into the system. Can you access real-time metrics on model drift, feature attribution, and prediction confidence scores? Are there hooks to set automated alerts for performance degradation or fairness threshold breaches? These are not nice-to-have features but essential tools for maintaining operational compliance.

Require a full architecture review conducted by your security team before signing any contract. This review should validate the vendor’s claims about data isolation, access controls, and audit logging capabilities. Never accept a vendor’s whitepaper as sufficient evidence; your technical team must perform hands-on validation.

Dive into the specifics of their feature store and model registry. A well-architected system will have immutable versioning for both data and models, allowing you to precisely roll back to a previous compliant state if an update introduces risk. Inquire about the accessibility of their explanation interface; it should allow your compliance team to input a specific decision and retrieve a counterfactual explanation (e.g., 'Why was this loan application denied?'). This granular, on-demand transparency is a hallmark of a trustworthy architecture built for scrutiny, not just performance.

Negotiate contractual safeguards and liability clauses

The procurement contract is your primary enforcement tool. Move beyond standard terms to include AI-specific clauses. These must clearly define model performance benchmarks, service levels for accuracy and fairness, and remedies, including financial penalties, for failing to meet these defined standards.

Liability must be explicitly addressed. Given the uncertain regulatory landscape, indemnification clauses should protect your organisation from fines and penalties arising from the vendor’s failure to comply with asserted regulations. Stipulate that the vendor is responsible for all costs associated with updating their system to meet new legal requirements.

Crucially, contract for a clear exit strategy. This includes clauses guaranteeing your continued access to the model API for a transition period post-termination and, critically, the right to receive a full copy of your historical inference data in a portable format. This prevents costly vendor lock-in and ensures business continuity.

A critical contractual safeguard is a clause for a 'Compliance Regression'. This stipulates that if a vendor update causes the model to fall out of compliance with your agreed-upon regulatory baseline, they are obligated to provide and fund a rollback to the last compliant version within a strict timeframe, such as 24 hours. Additionally, negotiate for the right to conduct your own independent audit upon a triggering event, like a regulatory change or a severe incident, with the vendor bearing the cost. This shifts the financial incentive onto them to maintain continuous compliance.

Conduct a structured proof of concept

A proof of concept must test more than just accuracy. Design your PoC scenarios to explicitly stress-test the model for regulatory compliance. Use edge cases and adversarial examples that could trigger fairness or safety concerns. Measure performance against your predefined compliance metrics, not just business outcomes.

During the PoC, audit the vendor’s support responsiveness and transparency. When an issue is identified, do they provide deep technical explanations and logs, or do they offer vague assurances? Their willingness and ability to collaborate on problem-solving during the PoC is a strong indicator of the post-sale partnership quality.

Based on the PoC results, produce a go/no-go recommendation that weighs both technical performance and compliance risk. This artefact should be presented to your organisation’s AI governance board or risk committee for a final, objective approval, ensuring the decision is not made by the procurement team alone.

Structure your PoC to test failure modes, not just success. Deliberately feed the model edge cases and disputed historical decisions to audit its output. For instance, if evaluating a loan application model, input a previous case that was overturned on appeal. Does the AI's reasoning align with your human committee's final decision? Establish clear, quantitative pass/fail criteria upfront; for example, 'The model must achieve 95% consistency with our manual fairness audit results across all protected classes.' This transforms the PoC from a performance demo into a rigorous compliance stress test.

Implement continuous post-deployment monitoring

Vendor evaluation does not end at signing. Your compliance team must establish a programme for continuous monitoring of the live system. This involves tracking the model’s performance against the contractual benchmarks and, more importantly, scanning for emergent drift or bias that could create regulatory exposure.

Maintain an open channel with the vendor for incident reporting. Require them to notify you immediately of any retraining efforts, model updates, or discovered vulnerabilities. Your contract should stipulate strict timelines for this notification, ensuring your organisation is never the last to know about a potential compliance issue.

Schedule quarterly business reviews with the vendor focused solely on compliance and governance. These sessions should review audit logs, discuss upcoming regulatory changes, and reassess the risk profile of the application. This ongoing dialogue turns a one-time procurement event into a dynamic risk management partnership.

Implement a dashboard that tracks key compliance metrics, such as model drift, fairness metrics across protected attributes, and explanation quality score, alongside performance KPIs. Assign specific roles: a Data Scientist monitors drift, a Compliance Officer reviews fairness alerts, and a Legal officer is notified if explanation confidence drops below a set threshold. Establish a monthly review meeting where this data is presented against the original baseline. This continuous feedback loop ensures the model operates as certified and provides an audit trail for regulators, demonstrating active oversight.
