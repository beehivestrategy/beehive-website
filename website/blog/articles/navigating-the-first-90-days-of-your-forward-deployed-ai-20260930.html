In short · answer first

The first 90 days transform a successful pilot into a scalable managed service. This period is dedicated to formalising the operating model, embedding your team, and initiating the first production workflows. Success is measured by achieving specific, pre-agreed operational and value milestones before full-scale rollout.

Key takeaways
  • Establish a joint governance council within the first two weeks.
  • Define and track three key value metrics from day one.
  • Complete the first knowledge transfer session by day 45.
  • Execute a full environment failover test by day 75.

Weeks 1-2: From Pilot Validation to Programme Launch

Your initial fortnight is a deliberate transition from a proof-of-concept mindset to a production programme. The primary objective is to formally close the pilot phase with a signed-off outcomes report and immediately convene the programme's kick-off meeting. This session establishes the core governance council, comprising your senior business sponsor, IT lead, and our delivery principal, to ratify the foundational charter and initial 90-day roadmap.

Concurrently, our technical team executes the first infrastructure-as-code deployment into your pre-provisioned, sanctioned development environment. This move from a pilot sandbox to a governed platform is the first concrete step towards production readiness. We also initiate the mandatory security and architecture review processes with your internal teams to ensure compliance is baked in from the outset, not bolted on later.

The key deliverable of this period is a living programme dashboard. This artefact becomes your single source of truth, tracking workstream progress against the agreed roadmap, nascent value metrics, and potential risks. Its initial setup, using your preferred tooling like Power BI or Tableau, ensures visibility and accountability for both teams from the very beginning.

A critical artefact to develop in this phase is the Joint Programme Charter. This document, co-signed by executive sponsors from both your organisation and the AI partner, formally establishes the programme's mission, scope, key performance indicators (KPIs), and the joint governance council's membership. It should explicitly name the single point of contact (SPOC) for technical queries, change requests, and commercial discussions on both sides. This charter prevents scope creep and ensures alignment on objectives before any significant engineering resources are deployed, serving as the foundational agreement for all subsequent activities.

Formalising the Joint Operating Model and Governance

A清晰的 operating model is the cornerstone of a successful managed service. Days 15 to 30 are dedicated to codifying how our teams will work together through a ratified operating level agreement (OLA). This document details meeting cadences, escalation paths, decision rights, and communication protocols, moving beyond ad-hoc pilot collaboration to a predictable, professional rhythm.

The joint governance council will meet weekly during this stabilisation phase. Its mandate is to provide strategic direction, remove organisational blockers, and approve any scope adjustments based on early learnings. We document all decisions in a shared register, linking them directly to affected roadmap items to maintain rigorous traceability for all stakeholders.

A critical output is the RACI matrix for the first two production use cases. This clarifies who is responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed for each tactical activity, from data pipeline execution to model retraining triggers. Establishing these roles early prevents confusion and ensures your internal teams are seamlessly integrated into the new workflow.

To operationalise governance, establish a clear RACI matrix for all key processes. For instance, the AI partner is typically Responsible and Accountable for model retraining and performance drift monitoring, while your internal IT team is Consulted on infrastructure impacts, and business unit leaders are Informed of outcomes. A practical tool is a shared backlog in Jira or Azure DevOps, where use case requests from business units are formally submitted, tagged with estimated value and effort, and prioritised by the joint governance council during bi-weekly syncs. This moves decision-making from ad-hoc requests to a structured, value-driven process.

The First Value Delivery: Selecting Initial Use Cases

The managed service must demonstrate tangible value within the first 90 days to secure ongoing sponsorship. We collaboratively select the first one or two production use cases based on a strict criteria: high business impact, low implementation complexity, and clear data availability. The goal is a quick, definitive win, not a multi-year transformation.

For example, an initial use case might be automating a specific, high-volume customer service enquiry classification rather than overhauling the entire contact centre. We define a precise value baseline—such as the current handling time and cost—against which we will measure the automated solution's performance after deployment.

Each chosen use case is broken down into a granular delivery plan owned by a dedicated workstream lead. Your product owner provides prioritisation, while our engineers handle the build. This partnership ensures the solution is not just technically sound but also delivers against a well-understood business pain point.

Employ a weighted scoring framework to objectively compare potential use cases. Criteria should include quantitative business value (e.g., estimated annual savings or revenue increase), implementation complexity (data availability, integration points), and strategic alignment (e.g., supports a top-three corporate initiative). For example, a use case scoring 9/10 on value but requiring a new data pipeline with a 12-week lead time might be deprioritised in favour of one scoring 7/10 that uses readily available data for a quicker win. This disciplined approach ensures the initial projects have the highest probability of demonstrable success, building crucial early momentum for the programme.

Knowledge Transfer and Building Internal Capability

A core objective of a forward-deployed model is to uplift your team's internal AI capability. We initiate structured knowledge transfer (KT) sessions from day 31 onwards. The first session focuses on the solution architecture, walking your engineers through the design patterns and infrastructure choices made during the pilot and their justification.

Subsequent KT modules are scheduled around key development milestones, such as after the first model training run or the initial data pipeline build. These are hands-on, collaborative workshops, not presentations. We document all processes in your company’s chosen wiki, creating a growing repository that your team can reference long after our engagement.

We advocate for a ‘see, do, teach’ model. Your designated engineers first observe our work, then pair with us on tasks, and finally are expected to run a procedure themselves with our supervision. This progressive approach builds confidence and ensures a smooth eventual transition of ownership for specific components.

Beyond generic training, mandate that the partner's data scientists deliver a formal 'Model Handover Package' for each initial use case. This artefact must include the model's code repository, a detailed data lineage report, a list of features used in training, model performance benchmarks, and a 'decision log' explaining key algorithmic choices. Simultaneously, your lead data engineer should be tasked with shadowing the deployment process to document the exact steps for containerisation, registry management, and CI/CD pipeline triggering. This creates the essential playbook for your team to eventually take over maintenance and replicate the process for future models independently.

Establishing Rigorous Monitoring and Value Tracking

What gets measured gets managed. We operationalise the value-tracking framework defined in the pilot's business case. This involves instrumenting the first production workflows to automatically log the agreed key performance indicators (KPIs), such as process automation rate or reduction in manual effort hours.

A weekly value dashboard is produced, juxtaposing these operational KPIs against the projected business outcomes, like cost savings or revenue protection. This creates a direct line of sight from technical deployment to financial impact, providing clear evidence for the programme’s return on investment to your finance team.

Beyond business metrics, we implement rigorous MLops monitoring for the AI models themselves. This tracks predictive performance, data drift, and system latency, providing early warning alerts if the model degrades in the live environment. This proactive stance ensures reliability and maintains trust in the AI's outputs.

Implement a dashboard that tracks both system health and business value KPIs. Technical metrics like model latency, throughput, and drift (using a metric like Population Stability Index) should be monitored automatically with alerts sent to the partner's MLOps team. Business metrics, such as the reduction in manual processing hours or the increase in conversion rates, must be owned by the business lead, who is responsible for validating the data weekly against operational reports. Establish a formal review every fortnight where the joint team analyses trends, and any KPI deviation exceeding 10% triggers an immediate root-cause analysis and corrective action plan.

Preparing for Scale: Security, Compliance and Infrastructure

Long-term scale requires a relentless focus on foundational integrity. We work with your cybersecurity team to complete a threat-modelling exercise for the initial use cases, identifying and mitigating potential vulnerabilities before they can be exploited. All findings are documented in a risk register and addressed according to your organisation's severity classifications.

Furthermore, we assist your compliance office in completing the required internal assessments for deploying an AI system. This often includes creating model cards, fairness audits, and documentation for the right to explanation. These artefacts are essential for meeting regulatory standards and are best developed iteratively alongside the build.

On the infrastructure front, we automate the deployment pipeline to enable seamless promotions of code from development to staging environments. By day 75, we execute a full failover test of the entire system to a secondary region to validate disaster recovery procedures. This proves the operational resilience of the service before traffic increases.

Initiate a security and compliance review by Week 6, conducted by your internal Infosec team using a standardised checklist. Key items include validating the partner's container vulnerability scanning reports, ensuring all data egress is logged and audited, and confirming that model APIs enforce strict authentication (e.g., via JWT tokens). Furthermore, draft a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) for the infrastructure team, detailing the process to provision a new environment, which includes whitelisting the partner's CI/CD IP addresses, allocating Kubernetes namespace quotas, and configuring network policies to restrict traffic to only necessary ports and services.

The Day 90 Review: Gatekeeping the Managed Service

The 90-day mark serves as a formal gate review before committing to the ongoing managed service. We prepare a comprehensive review pack for your steering committee, summarising progress against the initial roadmap, demonstrated value metrics, absorbed knowledge transfer, and results of compliance and resilience tests.

This review is not a retrospective but a forward-looking decision point. The pack includes a proposal for the next phase, outlining the use case pipeline, anticipated resource requirements, and a refined value forecast based on actual performance data. This allows for an informed, data-driven conversation about the programme's future scope and investment.

A successful review results in the formal sign-off to proceed into the steady-state managed service agreement. The governance council may also approve a revised strategic roadmap based on the lessons learned, ensuring the service continues to evolve in lockstep with your organisation's priorities.

The review must be based on a pre-defined Go/No-Go checklist. Key go criteria include: at least two initial use cases are live and have met their value KPIs for one full month; the joint governance model has functioned effectively for six consecutive weeks with documented decisions; and your team has successfully executed a pre-agreed technical procedure, like retrieving a new model prediction, without partner assistance. A 'No-Go' decision triggers a 30-day remediation plan with clear milestones. This objective gate ensures the transition to a managed service is based on proven capability and value, not just elapsed time.

Avoiding Common Pitfalls in the Transition Phase

Several recurring challenges can derail this critical transition. The most common is unclear decision rights, where your internal teams and our practitioners are unsure who has final authority on a change request. This is mitigated by the RACI matrix and weekly governance meetings, forcing clarity on accountability.

Another pitfall is extending the pilot's scope under the guise of the managed service without adjusting timelines or resources. Our joint dashboard includes a formal change control log. Any new request must be evaluated against its impact on existing priorities and value goals, preventing uncontrolled scope creep.

Finally, neglecting the cultural change required to operationalise AI can stifle adoption. We proactively identify and engage with process owners whose workflows will change, involving them in design reviews and training plans early. Managing this human element is as crucial as managing the technology for ultimate success.

A common pitfall is the 'black box' syndrome, where business users receive outputs but distrust them. Mitigate this by insisting on explainability features from day one. For a classification model, this could mean the API returns the top three influencing factors for each prediction (e.g., "Application denied due to: 1. Low credit score, 2. High debt-to-income ratio"). Create a simple UI for business users to query these explanations. This transparency is non-negotiable for building trust, facilitating user adoption, and ensuring ethical oversight, turning a potentially sceptical business unit into a champion for the AI system.
