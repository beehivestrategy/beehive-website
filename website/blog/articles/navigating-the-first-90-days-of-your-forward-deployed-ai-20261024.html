In short · answer first

The first 90 days transform your pilot into a production-ready system. This period establishes the operational rhythm, technical foundation, and stakeholder confidence required for long-term success. Your focus shifts from initial validation to building a repeatable process for value delivery and scaling.

Key takeaways
  • Establish a joint delivery cadence with weekly technical and monthly strategic reviews
  • Formalise a lightweight change control process for model iteration and data pipelines
  • Document operational procedures for incident response and model performance monitoring
  • Develop a six-month capability roadmap aligned to your core business objectives

Weeks 1-2: Transitioning from Pilot to Production

The immediate priority is formalising the working agreement that governs the managed service. This document, distinct from the commercial contract, details operational protocols: escalation paths, response time service level agreements for model drift, data pipeline failures, and security incident reporting. It assigns clear roles for your internal team and our delivery pod, preventing ambiguity during critical events.

Concurrently, we conduct a technical handover from the pilot team. This session captures all tacit knowledge, including data sourcing quirks, model training parameters, and initial user feedback. We inventory all artefacts—code, configuration files, documentation—and migrate them to your designated production environment, ensuring no institutional knowledge is lost in the transition.

This fortnight concludes with a kick-off meeting involving all key stakeholders. The agenda reviews the working agreement, presents the initial 90-day plan, and establishes the communication cadence. This meeting is crucial for aligning expectations and securing continued executive sponsorship for the operational phase ahead.

A critical artefact to finalize in Week 1 is the Production Readiness Review (PRR) checklist. This document, co-signed by your engineering lead and the service provider’s project manager, formally confirms that all infrastructure, security, and monitoring prerequisites are met. It should explicitly list approved deployment pipelines, validated alert severities (e.g., P1 alerts must page an on-call engineer), and the results of final load tests. This checklist acts as the contractual gate between the pilot's experimental phase and the full operational commitment, ensuring both parties have a shared understanding of what 'production' truly means for your specific use case and environment.

Weeks 3-4: Establishing the Operational Rhythm

A predictable delivery rhythm is now instituted. This includes a weekly technical sync where engineers review system performance, data quality metrics, and backlog refinement. A monthly business review presents key performance indicators to stakeholders, demonstrating value and aligning priorities. This rhythm creates stability and ensures continuous progress.

The first model monitoring alerts are configured and tested. We establish baselines for key metrics like prediction latency, data drift, and concept drift specific to your use case. A clear protocol is defined for who receives these alerts and the steps to investigate and remediate any issues, forming the basis of your AI governance.

We also onboard your nominated technical staff onto the monitoring and observability platforms. Through structured walkthroughs, they learn to interpret dashboards, access logs, and understand model behaviour. This knowledge transfer is fundamental to building internal capability and ensuring long-term operational resilience.

To effectively measure the operational rhythm, establish a weekly Operations Review Meeting (ORM). This 30-minute stand-up should include your product owner, a technical lead, and the provider’s account manager. The sole agenda is to review the previous week’s key performance indicators (KPIs) against the Service Level Objectives (SLOs) defined in your contract. For example, if model accuracy dipped below the 98% SLO, the discussion must focus on the root cause and the provider’s action plan. This meeting creates a consistent, data-driven forum for accountability and prevents minor issues from escalating due to neglect or miscommunication.

Weeks 5-6: Delivering the First Iterative Improvement

Based on initial production data and user feedback, we prioritise and execute the first model improvement cycle. This involves retraining the model on new data, fine-tuning hyperparameters, or implementing a minor feature enhancement. The change is deployed using a predefined, low-risk process to a staging environment first.

We measure the improvement's impact against a pre-agreed benchmark, such as increased prediction accuracy or reduced false positives. This demonstrates the managed service's ability to evolve and deliver increasing value, moving beyond maintaining the initial pilot solution to actively enhancing it.

The process for this change is documented as a standard operating procedure. This creates a reusable template for future iterations, ensuring each enhancement is delivered with consistent quality, proper testing, and clear measurement of its business impact.

The first improvement cycle is an ideal time to formalize your change management process. Define a simple but strict rule: all proposed model or feature changes must be accompanied by an A/B testing plan approved by your data science team. For instance, a proposed improvement to the natural language processing (NLP) model for better sentiment analysis should be deployed to a canary group of 5% of users first. The decision rule for a full rollout should be based on a statistically significant improvement in accuracy for that cohort, with no regression in inference latency. This prevents well-intentioned 'improvements' from inadvertently degrading the user experience at scale.

Weeks 7-8: Solidifying the Support and Escalation Framework

By now, the support process is stress-tested with real-world incidents. We refine the playbooks for common issues, such as data feed interruptions or a drop in model confidence scores. These playbooks detail step-by-step diagnostics and solutions, empowering your team to address routine events independently.

A major incident drill is conducted to validate the escalation path. We simulate a severe scenario, like a critical model failure, and run through the entire protocol: initial alert, internal triage, escalation to the dedicated delivery pod, and executive communication. This ensures everyone understands their role during a crisis.

The framework's effectiveness is reviewed with your team. We identify any bottlenecks or confusion and update the documentation accordingly. This collaborative review ensures the support model is robust and truly owned as a shared responsibility between our teams.

Beyond defining contact points, pressure-test the escalation framework with a tabletop exercise in Week 7. Simulate a severe incident, such as a 50% degradation in inference speed during peak business hours. Have teams walk through the entire process: from the first alert, to internal triage, to officially declaring a P1 incident and engaging the provider’s 24/7 support line with the required ticket severity. This drill validates communication channels, ensures everyone knows the required initial diagnostics to provide (e.g., current error logs, deployment version), and highlights any gaps in the runbooks before a real crisis occurs.

Weeks 9-10: Reviewing Performance and Business Alignment

We conduct a comprehensive performance review against the objectives set at the pilot's conclusion. This analysis moves beyond technical metrics to assess business outcomes, such as process efficiency gains or cost savings. The data is compiled into a formal value realisation report for stakeholders.

This report fuels a strategic roadmap session. We review the original use case and explore adjacent opportunities for AI deployment identified during the first two months. The discussion focuses on prioritising the next capability to develop, ensuring the programme's continued strategic relevance.

The output is a drafted six-month roadmap outlining the next phases of work. This plan balances iterative improvements to the existing system with the exploration of new, high-value capabilities, ensuring a pipeline of value extends well beyond the initial 90-day period.

The performance review must translate technical metrics into business value. Create a one-page dashboard for stakeholders that marries operational data with business outcomes. For example, pair the model's '99.2% uptime' metric with the '15% reduction in manual review tasks for the operations team' that it enabled. Similarly, link 'average inference latency of 200ms' to 'customer service satisfaction scores improving by 10 points due to faster response times'. This concrete translation justifies the investment, secures ongoing executive sponsorship, and ensures future roadmap planning is aligned with core business objectives, not just technical performance.

Weeks 11-12: Knowledge Transfer and Planning for Scale

A final, deep knowledge transfer session is conducted with your core team. This covers advanced topics like interpreting model explainability outputs, managing the retraining cycle, and configuring new alert thresholds. The goal is to deepen your team's operational confidence and self-sufficiency.

We collaboratively develop a scaling plan. This document outlines the resources, data requirements, and potential system architecture changes needed to handle 10x the current transaction volume or to expand the model to new business units. It is a pragmatic look at future growth.

The 90-day period concludes with a joint review of the entire engagement. We assess what worked well in our collaboration and identify areas for refinement. This retrospective ensures the next 90 days are even more effective and efficient, cementing a powerful partnership.

A key deliverable for knowledge transfer is the creation of a comprehensive 'Operational Handbook'. This living document, built in a shared wiki, should be populated by the provider’s team with detailed procedures for common tasks. This includes step-by-step guides for: restarting a stalled service pod, interpreting the most common alert types, and executing a controlled rollback to a previous model version. Each procedure must be validated by your team in a staging environment during Week 11. This handbook is the primary artifact that ensures your team’s self-sufficiency and reduces dependency on the provider’s support for routine operational actions.

Your Team's Role: A Checklist of Critical Internal Actions

Your success hinges on internal readiness. Assign a dedicated product owner who possesses both business authority and technical aptitude. This individual must champion the project internally, make priority decisions, and serve as the single point of truth for requirements and feedback throughout this period.

Ensure your data engineering team is engaged and available. Their collaboration is essential for resolving data pipeline issues, provisioning new data sources for model expansion, and upholding data quality standards. Their involvement cannot be an afterthought; it must be integrated into the operational plan.

Begin socialising the programme's early wins and learnings with other parts of the business. Identify potential evangelists in other departments who can see the applicability of AI to their challenges. This builds internal demand and paves the way for the scaling discussions in the final weeks.

Internally, your data science team must establish a continuous model monitoring protocol. This involves defining a weekly checklist to proactively detect model drift and data skew. Key actions include: comparing the statistical distribution of live production data against the training dataset using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, monitoring the volume of 'low confidence' predictions, and tracking the frequency of fallbacks to a default rule. Assigning an owner to this checklist ensures that performance degradation is identified early, often before it breaches an SLO, allowing you to task the managed service provider with a retraining cycle based on concrete, data-driven evidence.

Avoiding Common Pitfalls in the Initial Operational Phase

A frequent mistake is treating the managed service as a black box. Avoid disengaging after the kick-off. Proactive participation in reviews and deep involvement in incident analysis are what transform the service from a vendor relationship into a true technical partnership and capability builder.

Another pitfall is scope creep driven by early excitement. Adhere to the formalised change process established in the first month. New ideas are valuable but should be captured, assessed, and prioritised—not injected ad hoc—to protect the stability of the core production system and ensure deliberate progress.

Finally, do not neglect the cultural change. Use the early wins to tell a compelling story within your organisation about AI's value. Address team members' concerns about AI head-on through demos and education. Managing this human element is as critical as managing the technology itself.

A common but avoidable pitfall is the mishandling of new data pipelines. Teams often assume the managed service automatically ingests all new data sources. Establish a strict decision rule: any new data source must undergo a formal 'onboarding assessment' with the provider *before* integration. This assessment must confirm the provider's tooling supports the data format, validate that latency requirements can be met, and update the data privacy impact assessment. For example, attempting to integrate real-time video streams without this check could reveal incompatibility with the deployment environment, causing significant project delays and unexpected costs.
