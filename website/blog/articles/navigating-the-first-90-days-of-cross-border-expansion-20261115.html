In short · answer first

The first 90 days are a validation sprint, not a full-scale launch. Success hinges on testing your core value proposition with local buyers, establishing a minimal but compliant operational footprint, and converting initial pilots into referenceable revenue. This period is about de-risking the investment through rapid, evidence-based iteration.

Key takeaways
  • Establish a temporary market validation team within 14 days
  • Prioritise regulatory compliance and local payroll setup immediately
  • Secure three pilot customers before day 60 for early feedback
  • Conduct a formal go/no-go review before committing full investment

Decoding the first 90-day mandate

For mid-market enterprises, cross-border expansion is a high-stakes manoeuvre with constrained resources. The initial quarter is not about grand openings or maximum revenue; it is a structured validation phase. Your mandate is to pressure-test critical assumptions about product-market fit, channel viability, and operational scalability with minimal capital exposure before the board approves a multi-year investment cycle.

This timeframe demands a specific mindset shift from ‘launch’ to ‘learn’. Leadership must frame objectives around evidence gathering, not revenue targets. Key performance indicators should measure learning velocity: number of customer interviews conducted, pilot contracts signed, and regulatory hurdles cleared. This evidence forms the foundation for a credible, data-driven business case for full-scale operations beyond day 90.

Consequently, your team structure must be agile and temporary. Avoid immediately hiring a full country manager or large sales team. Instead, form a tiger team of internal commercial, product, and operational experts on short-term assignments. Their sole focus is to answer the fundamental question: can we acquire customers profitably and operate legally in this new territory?

A critical artefact for this phase is the 'Market Entry Hypothesis' document. This one-page summary should clearly state your assumptions on the target customer profile, the core value proposition for that market, the primary competitor you will displace, and the key regulatory hurdles you anticipate. This document becomes the foundational thesis against which all subsequent validation is measured. It should be signed off by all key stakeholders before the landing team is dispatched to ensure absolute alignment on what success looks like and what you are setting out to prove or disprove in the initial 90 days.

Week 0: Assembling your landing team

Before day one, formally charter a cross-functional landing team with a 90-day mandate. This group typically comprises a commercial lead, a product or service delivery specialist, and an operations lead. Each member is seconded from their primary role and granted decision-making authority for this sprint. Their first deliverable is a validated market entry hypothesis document.

The commercial lead identifies and contacts potential early adopters. The product lead adapts the core offering for local compliance and customer needs. The operations lead navigates legal entity registration, tax obligations, and initial banking arrangements. This team operates on a daily stand-up rhythm, reporting weekly to an executive sponsor to remove blockers instantly.

Crucially, this team is not the future country leadership. Their role is investigative and temporary. By day 45, they must provide a recommendation on the requisite profile for a permanent country manager, based on the validated operational and commercial challenges. This prevents premature and costly senior hires based on assumptions, not evidence.

Beyond core functions, the team must include a 'Regulatory Liaison'—a role often filled by a locally hired legal consultant or a internal subject matter expert on secondment. This individual is solely responsible for navigating the bureaucracy of business registration, tax compliance, and industry-specific licensing. Their first deliverable should be a detailed regulatory checklist and timeline, mapping every necessary approval and its typical processing duration. This prevents the critical path from being blocked by unexpected legal delays and ensures the commercial team can operate with a fully compliant entity from day one.

Days 1-30: Validating demand and regulatory footing

The first month is dedicated to concurrent regulatory reconnaissance and customer discovery. The operations lead engages a local corporate services firm to navigate entity setup, while the commercial lead initiates a blitz of prospect meetings. The goal is to secure letters of intent or pilot contracts, not full-year orders, to prove willingness to pay.

Customer conversations must test specific hypotheses: does our pricing model resonate, which features are most valued, and what are the key procurement triggers? Document every interaction in a shared tracker, noting objections and required adaptations. Simultaneously, begin the process of registering a legal entity, as this can often take four to six weeks to complete.

By day 30, the team should have a clear view of the regulatory pathway and have a minimum of five qualified prospects expressing strong intent. This evidence is critical for the first major checkpoint with leadership. Any significant roadblocks—such as insurmountable compliance barriers or a complete lack of product interest—should trigger an immediate strategic review.

Demand validation requires a disciplined approach to customer discovery. The landing team should conduct at least 30 structured interviews using a standardized questionnaire focused on the problem, not your solution. Key questions must probe current workarounds, the perceived cost of the problem, and decision-making authority. All findings should be logged in a centralized 'Validation Log' (e.g., a shared spreadsheet), tagging each assumption as 'Confirmed', 'Inconclusive', or 'Invalidated'. A decision rule must be established upfront: if more than 40% of core value proposition assumptions are invalidated by Day 30, the expansion strategy must be formally re-evaluated.

The initial commercial pipeline: quality over quantity

Building a robust pipeline is the core commercial activity, but its composition is unique to this phase. Prioritise prospects that offer strategic learning value, such as those in your ideal customer profile who represent a new vertical or use case. These early conversations are qualitative research sessions disguised as sales calls.

The primary objective is to understand the local buyer’s journey, budget cycles, and competitive landscape. Use a simple scoring matrix to prioritise leads based on their potential as a reference account, their strategic value for learning, and their short-term revenue potential. This ensures the team focuses its limited bandwidth on the most informative opportunities.

Aim to secure two to three pilot customers by the end of month two. These are not full contracts but paid proof-of-concept engagements. Their purpose is twofold: to generate initial revenue that funds local operations and to provide a real-world test environment for your service delivery and support model before scaling.

To ensure quality, implement a 'Tiered Prospect Framework' for the initial pipeline. Tier 1 prospects are not just interested; they have a clearly defined problem your solution addresses, a allocated budget, and a publicly stated strategic initiative that aligns with your offering. These should constitute at least 60% of the initial pipeline. The lead qualification checklist must require evidence for each criterion, such as a reference to a published annual report objective or a confirmed meeting with a economic decision-maker. This rigorous filtering prevents the team from chasing low-probability opportunities that distort early performance metrics.

Days 31-60: From pilot contracts to operational reality

With initial customer validation secured, the focus shifts to executing flawlessly on your first deliveries. This tests your operational assumptions about logistics, local support, and contract management. Assign a dedicated project manager from the landing team to oversee each pilot, ensuring rigorous documentation of the process and any challenges encountered.

This is also the period to establish your local financial operations. Open a business bank account, establish local payment processing, and implement a straightforward invoicing system. For payroll, consider using a global employer-of-record service to quickly hire one or two local support staff without establishing a full legal entity immediately.

The data generated from these first live transactions is invaluable. It validates your cash collection cycle, reveals hidden transaction costs, and tests customer acceptance of your payment terms. This operational evidence is as critical as the commercial pipeline in determining the long-term viability of the venture.

The transition from a signed pilot contract to a live customer requires a meticulous 'First Delivery Checklist'. This artefact should cover all operational facets: securing customer data in a compliant manner, configuring the product to the specific local market requirements (e.g., language, currency, data sovereignty laws), onboarding the client's team, and establishing a local support protocol. Assign an 'Operational Lead' to own this checklist and hold a daily stand-up during the first implementation to rapidly unblock any issues. The goal is to transform the initial contract into a tangible referenceable success story.

The day 60 strategic check-in

By the end of month two, convene a formal review with all key stakeholders and your executive sponsor. This is a go/no-go checkpoint. The landing team presents its findings across three domains: commercial viability, operational feasibility, and regulatory compliance. The presentation must be data-driven, featuring customer testimonials, pipeline metrics, and a revised financial model.

The outcome of this meeting is a decisive resource allocation decision. Green light means proceeding to build out the team for a full launch. Amber light might mean a 30-day extension to overcome a specific hurdle. Red light means a orderly wind-down of the pilot, having spent a fraction of the full expansion budget.

This disciplined approach prevents sunk cost fallacy from setting in. It forces a objective assessment of progress against the initial hypotheses. The greatest success is sometimes a swift ‘no’, saving the organisation from a far more costly failure six months later. The goal is intelligent de-risking.

The Day 60 check-in must produce a definitive 'Go/No-Go/Adapt' decision for the full-scale investment. The leadership team should review a standardized dashboard featuring: customer acquisition cost versus plan, pilot contract conversion rates, operational delivery timelines, and updated regulatory risk assessments. A pre-defined decision rule might be: if the validated market size is 50% or less of the initial hypothesis, it's a 'No-Go'. If operational costs are 25% higher but conversion rates are strong, it's an 'Adapt' scenario, requiring a revised operational model and budget before proceeding into the scaling phase.

Days 61-90: Scaling the beachhead

Assuming a green light, the final month is about transitioning from a project to a permanent operation. Begin recruiting the first full-time country manager, using the detailed profile developed by the landing team. The commercial lead starts building a proper sales pipeline, while operations formalises the entity setup and hires local staff.

The landing team’s knowledge must be institutionalised. They create a comprehensive market launch playbook detailing everything from compliance checks and preferred vendors to customer onboarding scripts and common objections. This document becomes the foundation for the new country team, ensuring a smooth handover.

The tiger team disbands by day 90, having completed its mission. Their final report includes a detailed three-quarter roadmap for the new entity, based on validated learning rather than conjecture. This sets the permanent team up for success with a realistic set of targets and a clear understanding of the local market dynamics.

Scaling the beachhead requires a 'Localization Playbook' based on the lessons from the first pilots. This document codifies everything from marketing messaging that resonated most and compliance-approved sales contracts to configured software settings and support workflows. The playbook allows you to onboard additional local account executives and support staff efficiently without relying on the initial landing team for tribal knowledge. Simultaneously, begin recruiting the first country manager using the success criteria defined in the 90-day scorecard; this ensures a smooth transition from the landing team to permanent local leadership.

Measuring what matters: your 90-day scorecard

Traditional revenue targets are poor indicators of success in this phase. Instead, measure leading indicators that prove market validation. Track the number of qualified customer meetings completed, the percentage of prospects that converted to pilots, and the net promoter score of your first customers. These metrics prove demand.

Operationally, measure the time to complete entity registration, open a bank account, and hire the first local employee. These are proxies for bureaucratic friction. Financially, track the burn rate of the landing team and the revenue from initial pilots. The goal is a shallow cash curve, not profitability.

The ultimate metric is a binary decision: a confident go/no-go recommendation at day 60. This decision must be backed by a evidence dossier containing customer contracts, a validated operational plan, and a revised financial model. This scorecard ensures the entire organisation aligns on what constitutes a successful market validation sprint.

Beyond financial metrics, the scorecard must include leading indicators of sustainable growth. Track the 'Net Revenue Retention' of your initial pilot cohort to gauge long-term value and product-market fit. Measure the 'Local Brand Awareness' through targeted surveys that ask about unaided recall in your category. Critically, include a 'Regulatory Health' score, auditing your adherence to all local compliance requirements. A final, qualitative metric should be at least two referenceable customer case studies that can be used as social proof for the next wave of expansion, proving you have established a credible and stable beachhead.
