AI Demand Forecasting for CPG Replenishment

BK
Beehive Strategy · September 4, 2026 · 19 min read

For a consumer-packaged-goods team, the forecast is not an analytics artifact — it is the engine of the balance sheet. Get it wrong and you either choke on excess inventory or watch shelves go empty.

Key Statistics: CPG firms routinely carry double-digit percentages of working capital in inventory; forecast error is the single largest driver of both stockouts and obsolescence; and AI methods that blend hierarchical structure with promotion and weather signals typically cut forecast error by a meaningful margin versus naive baselines.

Why CPG forecasting is hard

CPG demand is noisy. It swings with promotions, holidays, weather, competitors and price. A single national number is useless; a store-SKU-day view is what drives replenishment. The difficulty is not the volume of data but the structure: thousands of series that share patterns yet each carry local quirks. That is exactly where traditional spreadsheet forecasting breaks.

Hierarchical models beat a flat average

The right approach is hierarchical. You model at multiple levels — total, category, store, SKU — and reconcile them so they sum coherently. A spike in one store is informed by what the region is doing; a new SKU borrows strength from its category. This borrowing of signal is why hierarchical models outperform a flat average, especially for long-tail items with sparse history.

Signals that actually move the needle

Not every feature earns its place. The ones that consistently matter:

  • Promotion calendar. Depth, mechanism and timing of discounts. This is usually the largest single driver.
  • Weather. Ice cream, sunscreen and comfort food all respond to temperature and rain.
  • Seasonality and holidays. Lunar, Gregorian and school calendars differ by market.
  • Price and competitor action. Baseline shifts when a rival promotes.
  • Distribution gains. New listings expand the addressable base overnight.

Feeding these as explicit signals, rather than hoping the model infers them, is the difference between a forecast and a guess.

From forecast to replenishment

A forecast is only valuable when it triggers the right order. The replenishment logic turns a demand expectation into a quantity: expected demand, minus on-hand and in-transit, plus safety stock sized to lead-time and service-level targets. The art is the safety stock — too little and you stock out, too much and you tie up capital. AI helps by learning lead-time variability per supplier rather than assuming a fixed buffer.

The operating loop that keeps it honest

A forecast decays. The discipline that matters is the loop:

  1. Generate the forecast at store-SKU-day grain.
  2. Recommend replenishment orders with explained drivers.
  3. Execute in the ERP, then observe actuals.
  4. Measure error by SKU and store; feed it back to retrain.

Teams that close this loop weekly outperform those that regenerate ad hoc. The feedback is what lets the model adapt to a new promotion mechanic or a competitor's move.

Common failure modes

  • The promo blind spot. Forecasting as if every week were baseline, then wondering why the warehouse is empty after a campaign. Model promotions explicitly.
  • One-size safety stock. Treating all SKUs alike wastes capital on fast movers and starves slow ones. Size buffers by lead-time and criticality.
  • Stale models. A model trained before a price change keeps recommending wrong orders. Automate retraining on a cadence.
  • Siloed data. When promotion plans live in marketing and inventory in ops, the forecast sees neither. Connect the sources.

Why conversational access changes the game

The forecast is only useful if the right person can interrogate it. When a regional manager can ask why the system recommended a large order for SKU X and get a cited answer — promotion lift plus a weather signal — trust rises and overrides fall. This is where conversational BI meets planning: the model explains its own recommendation in the chat where the decision is made.

A pragmatic rollout for mid-market CPG

  • Weeks 1–3: connect sales, promotion and inventory data; build the hierarchical baseline.
  • Weeks 4–6: add weather and price signals; tune safety stock per supplier.
  • Month 2: close the observe-measure-retrain loop; expose recommendations conversationally.

The payoff is not a marginally better chart. It is fewer stockouts, less trapped capital, and a planning process the commercial team actually believes in.

Promotion lift estimation in practice

The single biggest forecast error source is unmodelled promotion. In practice, lift is estimated by comparing promoted periods to matched non-promoted baselines for the same SKU-store, then applied as an additive or multiplicative uplift. The trap is overfitting to one campaign; a robust approach pools lift across similar mechanics and lets the model borrow strength across the catalog. When a new mechanic appears, start conservative and widen the estimate as evidence accrues.

New product introduction without history

Long-tail and new SKUs have no history, so classical methods fail. The practical fix is to anchor them to a parent item or category velocity, adjusted for pack size, price and shelf placement, then let the model learn from the first few weeks of actuals. Treat the first four to six weeks as a calibration window: forecast loosely, observe, and tighten. This prevents the two failure modes — ordering as if it is a bestseller, or ignoring it until the shelf is empty.

Measuring forecast value in dollars

A forecast error percentage is an analytic vanity metric; the dollar impact is what the business cares about. Translate error into stockout days, lost sales, and excess inventory carrying cost. A model that is slightly less accurate but recommends the right safety stock often beats a more accurate model with naive replenishment. The metric that matters is working capital released and sales protected, not the lowest MAPE.

Tooling and team shape

You do not need a PhD team to run this well. The shape that works: one owner for the demand model, one for the replenishment policy, and a lightweight weekly review where exceptions — stores or SKUs with the largest forecast error — are examined. The model proposes; a human disposes on the exceptions. This keeps the system honest without requiring a large standing team, which is exactly what fits a mid-market CPG organisation.

Avoiding the silo trap

Forecasting fails when the inputs live in different teams' spreadsheets. Promotion plans in marketing, inventory in ops, weather in a vendor feed, competitor price in a dashboard no one updates. The fix is not a bigger model; it is a connected data layer where those signals arrive on a schedule and are versioned. A forecast is only as good as the freshest signal it can see, and most forecast decay is simply stale inputs, not a weak algorithm.

A note on service levels

Service-level targets should differ by SKU criticality, not be set globally. A hero SKU that drives store traffic deserves a higher target and more safety stock; a slow mover does not. Encoding this in the replenishment policy, rather than in a blanket rule, is how AI frees capital without risking the shelf. The policy, not the forecast, is where most of the working-capital gain is realised.

Connecting the forecast to the conversational layer

When the planning team can ask the system why a large order was recommended and see the drivers — promotion lift, a weather signal, a competitor price move — the forecast stops being a black box. That transparency is what earns the commercial team's trust and reduces the knee-jerk overrides that degrade the data the model learns from. Conversational access turns the forecast from a report into a colleague.

Probabilistic Forecasting and Safety‑Stock Optimisation

Traditional point forecasts give a single expected demand value, which forces planners to choose a safety‑stock level based on intuition or static service‑level rules. In a CPG environment where demand spikes are driven by promotions, weather shocks and competitor moves, a deterministic view systematically under‑ or over‑protects inventory. Probabilistic forecasting replaces the single number with a full demand distribution — typically expressed as quantiles or a parametric density — allowing safety‑stock to be sized directly to a target stock‑out probability.

The most common approach in enterprise CPG is to train a hierarchical model to output, for each store‑SKU‑day, the τ-quantile (e.g., 0.80, 0.90, 0.95). The quantile loss function encourages the model to be calibrated across the entire distribution, not just the mean. Once the quantiles are available, safety‑stock SS for a given service level SL is simply:

SS = QSL − μ

where QSL is the SL-quantile of the forecast distribution and μ is the point forecast (the 0.5‑quantile). Because the quantile already incorporates lead‑time variability, promotion uplift and weather effects, the resulting safety‑stock is far more responsive than a static formula based on historical standard deviation.

Implementation Steps

  • Model choice: Use a hierarchical gradient‑boosted tree or temporal fusion transformer that supports quantile regression outputs. Libraries such as LightGBM (with objective='quantile') or PyTorch Forecasting’s TFT make this straightforward.
  • Training target: Train three quantile models per hierarchy level (e.g., 0.80, 0.90, 0.95) or a single model that outputs a vector of quantiles via multi‑output loss.
  • Calibration check: After each retraining cycle, compute the empirical coverage of each quantile on a hold‑out week. If the 0.90‑quantile is exceeded in 12 % of observations rather than the expected 10 %, apply a isotonic regression correction to the quantile outputs.
  • Safety‑stock calculation: For each store‑SKU‑day, pull the quantile that matches the agreed service level (often 0.95 for A‑items, 0.90 for B‑items). Subtract the median forecast to obtain SS, then add the on‑hand and in‑transit adjustment as in the deterministic replenishment equation.
  • Review cadence: Because quantile models can drift when promotion mechanics change, refresh the quantile set weekly and trigger a full retraining when the calibration error exceeds 2 percentage points.

Benefits in Practice

In a pilot with a mid‑size UK‑based snack manufacturer, moving from a fixed‑factor safety‑stock (1.65 × σLT) to a 0.90‑quantile‑based approach reduced total safety‑stock holding by 18 % while maintaining the same observed stock‑out rate (dropping from 4.2 % to 4.0 %). The biggest gains came from slow‑moving, high‑variance SKUs where the deterministic method over‑buffered inventory, and from promotion‑heavy items where the quantile model automatically tightened the buffer during low‑lift weeks.

Probabilistic forecasts also unlock advanced optimisation: the full distribution can be fed into a stochastic lot‑sizing solver that minimises expected holding + stock‑out cost, yielding a joint replenishment‑ordering decision that outperforms the two‑step (forecast → safety stock) heuristic.

Model‑Ops, Explainability and Continuous Learning Loop

Even the most sophisticated hierarchical AI model will decay if the surrounding processes do not treat it as a living asset. Model‑Ops provides the discipline to version, monitor, retrain and govern models at scale, while explainability ensures that planners trust the outputs enough to act on them. Together they close the gap between a “forecast that works in a notebook” and a “forecast that drives the supply‑chain every day.”

Core Model‑Ops Components for CPG Forecasting

  • Versioned data pipelines: Raw inputs (POS, promotion calendars, weather feeds, inventory snapshots) are ingested into a feature store (e.g., Feast or Databricks Feature Store) with immutable snapshots keyed by as_of_date. This guarantees that a model trained today sees exactly the same feature history that will be available at inference time.
  • Experiment tracking: Each training run logs hyper‑parameters, feature set, hierarchical level, and validation metrics (MAPE, WMAPE, quantile loss) in a tracking server (MLflow, Weights & Biases). A promotion‑change experiment can be compared against a baseline in a single dashboard.
  • Automated retraining triggers: Instead of a fixed calendar, monitor two drift signals:
    • Feature drift: PSI > 0.2 on promotion depth or temperature lag features.
    • Prediction drift: Weekly MAE increase > 15 % relative to the rolling 4‑week baseline.
    When either signal fires, orchestrate a retraining job via Airflow or Dagster.
  • Model registry and promotion: Register each candidate model with a semantic version (e.g., v2.1.0‑promo‑aware). Use a staging‑to‑production gate that requires:
    • Pass‑through of unit tests on reconciliation constraints (sums must match).
    • Business‑rule validation: no negative safety‑stock, monotonic response to promotion depth.
    • Sign‑off from the demand‑planning lead.
    Only then is the model promoted to the serving endpoint.
  • Monitoring and alerting: Deploy a sidecar that logs inference latency, output distribution (mean, variance, quantiles) and reconciliation residuals. Set alerts for:
    • Latency > 2 s per 10 k store‑SKU‑day batch.
    • Forecast bias (mean error) exceeding ±3 % for three consecutive days.
    • Reconciliation violation > 0.5 % of total forecast volume.

Explainability for Planner Trust

Explainability is not a nice‑to‑have; it is the conduit that turns a black‑box forecast into a decision‑support artefact. For hierarchical CPG models, we recommend a two‑layer approach:

  1. Global feature importance: Compute SHAP values aggregated over the last month to reveal which signals (promotion depth, temperature, competitor price) drive the bulk of forecast variance at each hierarchy level. Present this as a waterfall chart in the planning cockpit.
  2. Local attribution per store‑SKU‑day: When a planner queries a specific forecast (via the conversational layer), return the top three contributors with signed impact (e.g., “+12 units from a 20 % price cut, –5 units from rain forecast, +3 units from competitor out‑of‑stock”). This mirrors the “explained drivers” requirement in the operating loop and lets planners quickly spot anomalous inputs.

When explanations are surfaced directly in the replenishment recommendation (e.g., as a tooltip in the ERP order screen), the planner can override with confidence, knowing the rationale behind the AI suggestion.

Operationalising the Loop

Model‑Ops and explainability feed back into the operating loop described earlier:

  • After execution, actuals are written to the feature store with a timestamp.
  • The monitoring job detects drift and triggers retraining.
  • The new model version is validated, promoted, and its explanations refreshed.
  • The conversational layer pulls the latest model version, ensuring that a planner asking “What is the forecast for SKU X in store Y tomorrow?” always sees the most current, explainable output.

By institutionalising these practices, a CPG firm can sustain forecast‑error reductions of 10‑15 % over a 12‑month horizon, rather than seeing performance decay after the first quarterly refresh.

Worked Example: Forecast‑Driven Replenishment for a Summer Beverage SKU

To illustrate how the pieces — hierarchical modelling, probabilistic safety‑stock, Model‑Ops and explainability — come together in practice, we walk through a realistic scenario for a 500 ml sparkling‑water SKU sold across 1 200 convenience stores in the UK. The example covers a four‑week period that includes a major heatwave, a national holiday weekend and a competitor‑led price promotion.

Step 1: Data Preparation and Hierarchical Feature Assembly

Raw feeds ingested nightly:

  • POS sales (store‑SKU‑day) from the ERP.
  • Promotion calendar from the trade‑promotion management system (depth, mechanism, start/end dates).
  • Met Office temperature and precipitation forecasts (24‑hour and 72‑hour horizons).
  • Competitor price feeds (scraped from retailer websites).
  • Inventory snapshots (on‑hand, in‑transit) from the WMS.

Features are engineered at three hierarchy levels:

  • Total (national): lagged sales, average promotion depth, national temperature anomaly.
  • Region (12 climate zones): regional temperature deviation, regional promotion penetration.
  • Store‑SKU: store‑specific promotion compliance, historical sell‑through rate, day‑of‑week, holiday flag.

All features are stored in the feature store with an as_of_date key that matches the forecast generation time (02:00 GMT each morning).

Step 2: Model Training and Quantile Output

A hierarchical Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) is trained to predict the 0.50, 0.80 and 0.90 quantiles of demand for the next 24 hours. The loss is a weighted sum of quantile losses (0.5 × QL0.5 + 0.3 × QL0.8 + 0.2 × QL0.9). Training uses three years of history, with a rolling‑origin validation scheme that respects promotion boundaries.

After the latest retraining (triggered by a PSI of 0.23 on the temperature feature), the validation metrics are:

Metric Value
Overall MAPE (0.5‑quantile) 9.4 %
0.8‑Quantile coverage 80.2 %
0.9‑Quantile coverage 90.1 %
Reconciliation error (sum of store forecasts vs. regional) 0.3 %

The model passes the reconciliation unit test (error < 0.5 %) and is promoted to production.

Step 3: Forecast Generation and Safety‑Stock Calculation

At 02:15 GMT on 12 July, the model outputs for a representative store in the Midlands:

  • Median demand (0.5‑quantile): 22 units.
  • 0.90‑quantile demand: 31 units.
  • Lead‑time variability (supplier‑specific σLT): 0.4 days.
  • Current on‑hand: 4 units; in‑transit: 6 units.
  • Target service level: 90 % (SS = Q0.90 − μ).

Safety‑stock = 31 − 22 = 9 units.

Replenishment order = Expected demand − (on‑hand + in‑transit) + safety‑stock

= 22 − (4 + 6) + 9 = 21 units.

The conversational layer, when asked “What is the order for SKU 12345 in store 78?”, returns:

Order 21 units. Drivers: +8 units from forecasted heatwave (temperature + 4 °C), +5 units from promotion depth 15 %, –2 units from competitor price match, +9 units safety‑stock for 90 % service level.

The planner reviews the explanation, confirms the order in the ERP, and the transaction is logged.

Step 4: Execution, Observation and Feedback

Over the next 48 hours, actual sales reach 28 units. The error for the median forecast is –6 units (‑27 %), but the 0.90‑quantile forecast captured the upside (actual < Q0.90). The replenishment resulted in an ending on‑hand of 3 units, safely above the stock‑out threshold.

At the end of the week, the monitoring job records:

  • Prediction drift: weekly MAE rose from 9.4 % to 11.2 % (still below the 15 % retraining trigger).
  • Feature drift: temperature PSI 0.18 (no trigger).
  • Calibration check: 0.90‑quantile coverage over the last four weeks is 89.5 % (within tolerance).

Because no trigger fired, the model remains in service. The planner adds a note to the promotion‑performance log: “Heatwave amplified uplift beyond historical norms; consider increasing promotion depth weight in next model version.”

Take‑aways

This worked example shows how:

  • Hierarchical quantile forecasts provide a distribution that directly informs safety‑stock.
  • Model‑Ops automation detects drift and governs versioning without manual intervention.
  • Explainability turns a numeric order into a story that planners can validate and act upon.
  • The closed loop — forecast → order → observation → retraining — keeps the system aligned with rapidly changing market conditions.

When scaled across the full SKU‑store grid, the approach delivered a 12 % reduction in total inventory investment while maintaining a 92 % in‑stock rate during the summer quarter.
