In short · answer first

Begin by establishing a cross-functional AI governance board. Then, implement a mandatory inventory for all AI systems, categorising them by risk. Develop clear procedural checkpoints for development and procurement. Finally, create a continuous monitoring and audit programme. This structured approach ensures compliance and builds trust.

Key takeaways
  • Establish a cross-functional AI governance board with clear mandates
  • Create a mandatory inventory for all AI systems, categorised by risk
  • Implement procedural checkpoints for development and procurement cycles
  • Build a continuous monitoring and audit programme for compliance

Establish your AI governance board and mandate

Your first practical step is to form a cross-functional AI governance board. This is not a technical committee but a strategic one. Its core membership must include Legal, Compliance, Risk, Data Privacy, and key business unit leaders. This group holds the authority to approve AI use cases, set policy, and manage exceptions, ensuring governance is embedded across the enterprise from the outset.

The board’s mandate must be explicitly documented in a charter. This charter should clearly define its scope, decision-making authority, and escalation paths. Crucially, it must outline the board’s responsibility for upholding the company’s AI ethics principles. This document becomes your foundational artefact, providing the necessary legitimacy and clarity for the board to operate effectively and make binding decisions.

To ensure operational effectiveness, schedule monthly meetings with a standardised agenda. This agenda should routinely review new AI project proposals, assess ongoing initiatives for compliance drift, and review incident reports. This rhythm creates a predictable governance cadence, integrating oversight into the business lifecycle rather than treating it as a separate, after-the-fact audit function.

The board’s charter must explicitly define its authority and decision-making protocols. This includes a RACI matrix for key decisions, such as approving high-risk AI use cases or halting deployments. For instance, the board might operate on a consensus model for major decisions but delegate authority to a designated ‘AI Risk Officer’ for time-sensitive, lower-risk approvals. The charter should also mandate the creation of quarterly reports for the executive committee, detailing the state of AI governance, incident summaries, and progress against the strategic roadmap, ensuring continuous executive oversight and support.

Build a mandatory AI system inventory

Concurrently, mandate the creation of a centralised inventory of all AI and automated decision systems. This is your single source of truth. Require every business unit to disclose systems they use, whether developed in-house, procured from a vendor, or accessed via an API. This initial discovery phase is critical for understanding your exposure and prioritising efforts.

For each system in the inventory, you must capture specific metadata. Essential fields include the system’s purpose, its data sources, the business owner, and the technical owner. Most importantly, you must record its risk category based on a predefined impact assessment. This structured data collection transforms an amorphous list into an actionable governance asset.

This inventory is not a static register; it is a living document. Implement a process requiring that any new AI system, prior to deployment, must be registered. Likewise, decommissioning must be recorded. This ensures ongoing visibility and prevents shadow AI projects from emerging outside the governance framework, maintaining a complete and accurate overview.

The inventory process must be supported by a standardized data collection form to ensure consistency. This form should capture essential metadata such as the system’s common name, its unique internal identifier, the business process it supports, the owning department, and the primary vendor (if procured). To automate discovery, integrate this process with cloud service management tools and code repositories, using tags like ‘AI-Model’ to flag potential systems. A quarterly automated reconciliation check should be run against cloud expenditure to identify any unaccounted-for compute resources that might indicate a ‘shadow AI’ project.

Implement a risk-based classification framework

With your inventory populated, apply a consistent risk classification framework to each system. We recommend a multi-factor model assessing impact on individuals, operational resilience, and reputational risk. For example, a customer-facing chatbot would be classified differently from an internal logistics optimisation tool. This triage allows for proportionate governance controls based on actual risk.

Define at least three tiers, such as High, Medium, and Low risk. The criteria for each tier must be concrete and decision-ready. A high-risk classification could be triggered by factors like processing sensitive personal data or making significant automated decisions. Publish these decision rules to ensure consistent and objective categorisation across the organisation.

This classification directly dictates the level of scrutiny required. High-risk systems will necessitate a full impact assessment, external audit, and mandatory board approval. Medium-risk systems may require a lighter-touch assessment and internal audit. Low-risk systems might only need basic registration. This risk-proportionate approach allocates scarce compliance resources efficiently.

A practical decision rule for the ‘High-Risk’ category could be: "Any system that autonomously makes legal or material financial commitments, or performs large-scale profiling for sensitive attributes, is automatically classified as High-Risk." For example, a chatbot offering personalized financial advice would be High-Risk, whereas an internal tool summarizing meeting transcripts would be Low-Risk. This framework must be documented in a readily accessible policy document, accompanied by a simple digital checklist that guides users through the classification questions, automatically suggesting a tier based on their answers.

Integrate checkpoints into development and procurement

Governance must be woven into the fabric of your processes. For in-house development, integrate mandatory checkpoints into your existing software development lifecycle. The first checkpoint should be a feasibility review at the ideation stage. The most critical is a gate review before deployment, where proof of compliance is required to proceed.

For procurement, update your vendor due diligence questionnaire to include specific AI and data ethics clauses. Require vendors to disclose their model’s training data, explainability features, and performance metrics. Make contract signing contingent on their system passing your internal risk assessment and being registered in your inventory. This shifts compliance upstream.

The artefact for these stages is a standardised compliance pack. For each project, this pack should contain the completed impact assessment, model cards, data sheets, and proof of testing. This creates a clear audit trail and ensures no system can advance without demonstrating it has met the necessary governance requirements.

A key procurement checkpoint is the Vendor AI Assessment Questionnaire, a due diligence artefact covering the vendor’s own governance, data provenance, model testing protocols, and breach notification policies. Internally, the ‘Model Readiness Checklist’ is a gatekeeping artefact requiring sign-off from legal, security, and compliance representatives before a model can be deployed to a staging environment. This checklist confirms that impact assessments are complete, data rights are secured, and monitoring hooks are in place, preventing any system from advancing without fulfilling its compliance requirements.

Conduct mandatory impact assessments

For any high-risk AI system, a formal impact assessment is non-negotiable. This is a deep-dive document that goes beyond the inventory. It must systematically evaluate the system’s potential impact on fairness, transparency, privacy, and safety. The assessment should be led by the project team and reviewed by the governance board.

The assessment template should force concrete answers. It must document the algorithm’s purpose, data provenance, and any identified biases. It should outline the measures taken to mitigate these risks, such as explainability techniques or human-in-the-loop safeguards. This process isn't about prohibition; it's about demonstrating diligent risk management.

The output of a completed assessment is a go/no-go recommendation for the governance board. It provides the evidence needed for an informed approval. It also serves as a living document that should be revisited if the system’s context changes significantly or after a set period, ensuring ongoing suitability.

The assessment report must document not just risks but also the specific mitigation strategies and their owners. For a recruitment tool, a finding might be: "Risk: Potential for gender bias in candidate ranking. Mitigation: Implement regular disparity testing on hired vs. rejected candidates. Owner: Head of Talent Acquisition." The assessment should also include a concrete plan for human oversight, specifying the role (e.g., a loan officer) responsible for reviewing a defined percentage of the AI’s automated decisions, ensuring accountability is baked into the operational process from the start.

Deploy continuous monitoring and audit protocols

Approval for deployment is not the finish line. You must establish continuous monitoring protocols for live AI systems. This involves tracking key performance indicators for model accuracy and fairness drift over time. For example, a drop in model performance for a specific demographic group would trigger an alert for investigation.

Schedule annual audits for high-risk systems. These audits should be conducted by an independent internal audit team or a third party. They must verify that the system operates as described in its impact assessment and that data handling complies with both internal policy and external regulations like the EU AI Act.

Maintain an audit log of all model changes and performance reviews. This log is a crucial artefact for demonstrating a history of diligent oversight to regulators. It shows that your governance is not a one-time exercise but an embedded, operational practice that actively manages risk throughout the system’s lifecycle.

Monitoring must track both performance metrics and fairness indicators. Define specific, auditable thresholds for model drift (e.g., "trigger a review if accuracy drops 5% from baseline") and fairness (e.g., "trigger an alert if the approval rate disparity between demographic groups exceeds 2%"). The audit protocol should include a schedule for third-party validation, particularly for high-risk systems. This involves providing auditors with access to model version histories, testing datasets, and a log of all triggered alerts and their resolutions to demonstrate a robust control environment.

Create clear roles, responsibilities, and training

Governance fails without clear accountability. Define and communicate specific roles. The Model Owner is a business leader accountable for the system’s outputs. The Technical Owner manages its performance. A Data Custodian ensures data quality and provenance. The Compliance Liaison acts as the bridge to the governance board. This RACI model prevents ambiguity.

Develop role-based training programmes. Business leaders and model owners need training on ethical principles and their accountability. Developers and data scientists require practical training on implementing fairness tests and explainability techniques. This upskilling ensures everyone possesses the knowledge to fulfil their responsibilities within the new framework.

Integrate these responsibilities into job descriptions and performance reviews. This signals the organisation’s serious commitment to responsible AI. It moves governance from an abstract policy to a tangible set of individual accountabilities, creating a culture where AI ethics and compliance are owned by everyone, not just a central team.

Beyond broad training, develop role-specific certification paths. A data scientist requires advanced training on bias detection and mitigation techniques, while a procurement officer needs expertise in evaluating vendor AI governance claims. Create a central ‘AI Governance Hub’ on the company intranet that houses all policies, templates, and training materials. This hub should clearly list subject matter experts for each domain (e.g., legal, security, ethics) and their responsibilities, making it easy for project teams to identify and engage the correct stakeholders early in the development lifecycle.

Maintain living documentation and prepare for audit

Your entire framework must be documented in a central, accessible playbook. This playbook should contain all policies, procedures, template artefacts, and role definitions. It acts as the definitive guide for anyone building, buying, or using AI within your company. Treat it as a living document, updated quarterly based on lessons learned.

Structure your documentation with an external regulator in mind. Assume everything will be reviewed. Ensure you can easily produce, for any system, a complete record: its inventory entry, impact assessment, monitoring reports, and audit findings. This organised evidence package is the ultimate output of an effective governance programme.

Finally, conduct a dry-run internal audit. Before any regulatory scrutiny, test your own framework. Try to assemble the compliance pack for a key system from scratch. This stress test will reveal gaps in record-keeping or process long before an external audit, allowing you to remediate issues and demonstrate confident, audit-ready compliance.

Living documentation is maintained through version control for all key artefacts, including model cards, datasheets, and assessment reports. Any change to a model or its training data should automatically create a new version of these documents, with the change log explicitly stating the reason for the update. To prepare for an external audit, maintain an ‘Audit Ready’ packet for each system. This is a compiled dossier containing its current classification, all approved versions of its impact assessment, monitoring reports, and proof of required board approvals, enabling a rapid and thorough response to auditor requests.
