In short · answer first

Build a multi-disciplinary AI Products Group, not a centralised lab. This team integrates applied scientists, ML engineers, and product managers directly with business units. Their core mandate is to abstract vendor-specific implementations, maintain a model inventory, and own a continuous evaluation framework, making vendor moves a managed operational event, not a crisis.

Key takeaways
  • Establish a cross-functional AI Products Group reporting to a business unit head
  • Mandate abstraction layers for all vendor APIs and model endpoints
  • Implement a continuous model evaluation and performance monitoring regime
  • Maintain a live model inventory with clear ownership and deprecation schedules

The Flaw in the Centralised AI Lab Model

The traditional approach of a centralised AI innovation lab, reporting to a CTO or CDO, is structurally unsuited for vendor volatility. This model creates a critical single point of failure. When a vendor changes its pricing, deprecates a model, or suffers an outage, the lab becomes a bottleneck. All business units depending on its services are paralysed, awaiting a solution from a team that is disconnected from immediate commercial pressures.

This central team often prioritises experimental new capabilities over operational resilience. Their performance metrics are typically based on prototype development and proof-of-concept delivery, not on system uptime or total cost of ownership. Consequently, building robust abstraction layers and contingency plans is not incentivised. The lab becomes a black box, obscuring vendor dependencies from the business teams that ultimately bear the risk.

The result is reactive firefighting. A model deprecation notice triggers a frantic, high-cost redevelopment effort led by specialists who lack deep context of the business use case. This disrupts roadmaps, inflates budgets, and erodes trust in AI capabilities. The enterprise is perpetually vulnerable to the whims of its external suppliers, with no strategic control over its own AI destiny.

The AI Products Group: A Resilient Operating Model

The antidote is the AI Products Group, a dedicated, cross-functional team embedded within a specific business unit or product line. This team owns the full lifecycle of AI-powered features, from conception to retirement. Its composition is critical: a product manager defines the problem, applied scientists select and tune models, and ML engineers handle deployment and monitoring, all working toward shared business outcomes.

This structure aligns incentives with resilience. The team’s success is measured by the reliability and business impact of its live services, not just project delivery. They feel the direct pain of a model degradation or API change, forcing proactive investment in mitigation strategies. This ownership model fosters deep, contextual knowledge of the specific use cases, data landscapes, and performance requirements.

Crucially, this model scales horizontally. Instead of one central bottleneck, you can have multiple AI Products Groups across different divisions, each specialising in their domain. This distributes risk and fosters internal best practice sharing. Governance shifts from a controlling central body to a enabling centre of excellence that provides tools, standards, and guidance, empowering the product groups to move with speed and safety.

Key Roles and Their Responsibilities in the New Model

The Applied Scientist is the team’s subject matter expert on model behaviour. Their core responsibility extends beyond initial selection to continuous evaluation. They establish a performance baseline for each live model and run regular checks against challenger models from other vendors. They define the key metrics for success and failure, creating the rules for when a model switch must be triggered.

The Machine Learning Engineer owns the infrastructure that makes volatility manageable. They architect and maintain the abstraction layer, such as a unified API gateway that routes requests to the appropriate vendor endpoint. They implement the monitoring and alerting for latency, cost, and output quality drifts. They are responsible for the technical execution of a model swap once a decision is made.

The Product Manager provides the commercial and operational context. They quantify the business impact of a model change, balancing cost, performance, and risk. They own the roadmap and communicate with stakeholders about potential disruptions. They are the ultimate decision-maker on whether to absorb a cost increase or initiate a switch, ensuring every technical decision is tied to a business outcome.

The Foundational Artefact: The Live Model Inventory

The first operational artefact the team must create and maintain is a live model inventory. This is a structured registry, not a static spreadsheet, that catalogs every model in production. Each entry must detail the vendor, the specific model name and version, the business service it powers, its cost structure, and its scheduled deprecation date if available.

Most importantly, each model must have a designated owner from the AI Products Group and a clearly documented service level objective. This defines the minimum acceptable performance threshold for that use case. The inventory should be integrated with monitoring tools to provide a real-time health dashboard, making the abstract concept of model performance tangible and actionable for the entire team.

This inventory transforms risk management from reactive to proactive. Teams can schedule quarterly reviews of models nearing end-of-life, budgeting and planning for transitions well in advance. It allows for impact analysis; a vendor announcement can be immediately cross-referenced with the inventory to identify all affected services, enabling a coordinated, prioritised response rather than chaotic discovery.

Architecting for Abstraction: The API Gateway Pattern

The primary technical defence against vendor lock-in is a deliberate abstraction layer. The most effective pattern is an internal API gateway that acts as the sole interface for all AI service calls. Application code integrates with this gateway using a standardised request format, not directly with vendor-specific SDKs. The gateway then handles the translation and routing to the actual provider.

This gateway must be designed to easily plug in new model providers. A configuration file, not code, should determine the routing logic. For instance, a feature flag could shift a percentage of traffic from Vendor A’s model to Vendor B’s model for a canary release. This makes A/B testing and gradual cutovers a routine operation, minimising downtime and risk during transitions.

This pattern also centralises critical cross-cutting concerns. The gateway is the logical place to enforce usage quotas, implement request caching, add custom logging, and mask prompt details for security. It provides a single point of visibility for all AI traffic, cost attribution, and performance data, which is invaluable for auditing and optimising your overall AI expenditure across multiple vendors.

The Continuous Evaluation Framework

Proactive teams do not wait for user complaints to signal a problem. They implement a continuous evaluation framework that automatically tests model outputs against predefined criteria. This involves maintaining a golden dataset of representative inputs and expected outputs for each use case. This dataset is run against the live model at regular intervals, say weekly, to detect regressions.

Evaluation goes beyond simple accuracy. It should include metrics for cost per query, latency, and custom business logic checks. For a content moderation model, this could include checks for false positives on approved content. For a summarisation model, it could check for hallucination rates. These automated checks provide an objective signal of model health decoupled from volatile production traffic.

The output of this framework is a continuous performance scorecard for each live model. This data is the primary input for the Applied Scientist’s analysis. A statistically significant drop in any key metric automatically triggers an alert to the team, kicking off a structured investigation process long before the issue affects a critical mass of end-users or becomes a business crisis.

A Decision Framework for Model Switching

When evaluation data signals a problem, a clear decision framework prevents analysis paralysis. The first step is triage: determine if the issue is a temporary vendor outage, a gradual performance drift, or a sudden, permanent degradation. This initial diagnosis dictates the response protocol, from waiting it out to immediately routing traffic to a fallback model.

For a gradual drift, the framework should mandate a structured challenger process. The team selects one or two alternative models from the inventory and routes a small percentage of traffic to them, comparing performance against the golden dataset and live metrics. The decision rule for a full switch is based on a composite score of performance, cost, and implementation effort.

The final decision is a go/no-go business call made by the Product Manager, informed by the scientists' data and the engineers' effort estimates. The framework pre-defines the communication plan for stakeholders and the rollback procedure in case the new model fails post-switch. This turns a high-anxiety event into a rehearsed, operational procedure with clear ownership.

Integrating Vendor Strategy into Your Planning Cycle

Resilient teams actively monitor vendor strategy, treating it as a key input to their own planning. This means assigning someone, often the Product Manager, to track announcements from key providers. They should analyse earnings calls and developer conference keynotes not just for features, but for strategic intent, such as a vendor pivoting to focus on large clients or specific industries.

This intelligence is integrated into quarterly business reviews. The team assesses its model inventory against the vendor landscape, asking strategic questions. Is our primary vendor making moves that align with our long-term needs? Are we over-invested in a vendor that is losing developer momentum? This forward-looking analysis identifies potential risks and opportunities long before a deprecation notice arrives.

This practice allows for strategic hedging. If dependency on a single vendor is deemed too risky, the team can proactively allocate a portion of its budget to building integration and testing a model from a competing vendor. This work is scheduled as a strategic initiative, not an emergency project, ensuring the enterprise always has a viable and tested exit strategy ready to deploy.
