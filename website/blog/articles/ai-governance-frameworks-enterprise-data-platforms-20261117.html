In short · answer first

The first 90 days transform a two-week pilot into a production-ready asset. This period is a structured proving ground to validate technical feasibility, demonstrate business value, and build the operational cadence for long-term managed services. Success is measured by a signed business case for scaling and a fully operationalised, continuously improving AI system.

Key takeaways
  • Establish a joint delivery cadence within the first week
  • Produce a concrete business case by day 60 to secure funding
  • Formalise a handover protocol from pilot to managed service team
  • Document all model performance and data lineage for compliance

The Strategic Imperative of the First 90 Days

The initial 90-day period following a two-week forward-deployed engineering pilot is not an extension of exploration; it is a structured bridge to production. This phase is designed to systematically de-risk the investment by transforming a promising proof-of-concept into a viable, measurable asset. Your primary objective is to validate the pilot's business hypothesis under real-world operating conditions and build an irrefutable case for scaling.

This timeline is deliberately constrained to maintain urgency and focus. It forces critical decisions about data pipelines, model performance, and integration architecture that often languish in traditional projects. The output is not merely a technical report but a production-ready system operating under a managed service agreement, complete with defined service-level objectives and a clear roadmap for its future evolution and ownership.

Failure to capitalise on this momentum results in pilot atrophy, where initial excitement fades and the project is classified as a research initiative rather than a business tool. Success, measured by a signed business case and operational handover, establishes a template for replicating AI delivery across the organisation, turning a single project into a strategic capability.

Weeks 1-2: Cementing the Pilot Foundation and Launching the Build

The first week is dedicated to formalising the working agreement and launching the build phase. This begins with a joint kick-off meeting involving your core business, IT, and compliance stakeholders and our delivery team. The goal is to ratify the project charter, which details objectives, key results, roles, and the agreed-upon cadence for stand-ups, reviews, and governance meetings.

Concurrently, our engineers begin refactoring the pilot code for production robustness. This involves setting up proper version control, implementing logging and monitoring hooks, and containerising the application environment. Your IT team's role is to provision the agreed-upon development and staging environments, ensuring network access and security group configurations are in place for a seamless deployment pipeline.

A critical artefact produced this week is the detailed data pipeline specification. This document maps the entire flow from source systems to model inference, highlighting any necessary data transformation, quality checks, and potential latency issues. It serves as the blueprint for building a reliable pipeline and is essential for future auditing and scaling efforts.

A critical artefact to finalize in Week 1 is the Joint Responsibility Matrix (JRM). This document explicitly defines ownership for each project component, such as data provisioning (client's data engineering team), model retraining (AI partner's ML engineers), and endpoint security (client's CISO office). The JRM prevents operational ambiguity and should be signed off by all team leads. Additionally, the initial technical stand-up should be established with a strict, pre-defined agenda focusing on build progress, impediments, and data quality metrics. This ensures the cross-functional team, from business SMEs to backend developers, is aligned from day one.

Weeks 3-6: Building the Production-Ready Data and Model Pipeline

This phase focuses on engineering the industrial-strength data pipeline identified in the initial specification. The team implements automated data extraction routines from source systems, ensuring they handle incremental loads and source system changes gracefully. Data quality checks are codified and integrated to flag anomalies before they pollute the model, building trust in the system's outputs from the start.

The pilot model undergoes rigorous validation against a hold-out dataset that was not used during development. Performance metrics are tracked against the baseline established in the pilot. More importantly, the model is monitored for concept drift and bias, establishing the foundational practice of continuous model evaluation that the managed service will later operate.

By the end of week 6, you should have a fully automated CI/CD pipeline that can build, test, and deploy the model to a staging environment. This automated process is a core deliverable, reducing manual effort and error, and providing the technical foundation for the managed service team to take over ongoing deployment and maintenance tasks efficiently.

A non-negotiable technical deliverable for Week 4 is the Data Validation Report. This automated report, generated by the newly built pipeline, runs daily and quantifies data drift, schema changes, and the percentage of null values in critical feature columns. For example, if a 'customer_tenure' feature suddenly contains 30% nulls, the report triggers an immediate alert to the data steward. This moves data quality from a subjective assessment to a governed, metric-driven process. Concurrently, the MLOps engineer should establish the baseline model's performance thresholds on the validation set, which will later serve as the benchmark for determining if a model regression warrants halting the deployment.

Weeks 7-8: Integration, User Acceptance Testing, and Operational Handover

The system is now integrated into a production-mirror staging environment. End-to-end testing is conducted to validate the integration with downstream applications and business intelligence tools. This is not just a technical test; it is a full user acceptance testing cycle involving a cohort of business users who validate the outputs against real-world scenarios and operational workflows.

Concurrently, the operational runbook is drafted and socialised with your IT operations team. This comprehensive guide details everything from routine monitoring procedures and alert responses to disaster recovery plans and escalation protocols. This document is a living artefact that ensures operational knowledge is transferred and not siloed with the development team.

A key milestone is the security and compliance review. The system is assessed against your organisation's internal policies and relevant regulatory requirements. Any gaps identified are remediated, and evidence of compliance is documented. This formal review is a prerequisite for granting authority to operate and move the system into a live production environment.

The User Acceptance Testing (UAT) phase requires a formal, pre-approved script executed by a cohort of 10-15 power users. This script should cover not only 'happy path' scenarios but also edge cases and common failure modes. For instance, a test case might be: 'Input a transaction value 3 standard deviations above the mean and validate the anomaly flag is triggered.' Each test case must be signed off by the business process owner. Furthermore, the operational handover checklist must include items like verifying access keys are rotated, all credentials are stored in a vault, and the client's IT team has successfully executed a documented rollback procedure for the API.

Weeks 9-12: Go-Live, Baseline Establishment, and Managed Service Transition

The go-live deployment is executed as a controlled launch, often using a feature flag or canary release strategy to minimise risk. Initial traffic is routed to the new system gradually while performance and business impact are closely monitored. The focus is on ensuring stability and observing user adoption rather than pushing for immediate scale.

The first two weeks of live operation are used to establish a performance baseline under real load. Key metrics—system latency, throughput, model accuracy, and business KPIs—are measured and documented. These baselines become the benchmark against which the managed service team's performance is measured, forming the basis of your service-level agreements.

Formal handover to the managed service team occurs. This involves knowledge transfer sessions, access provisioning to monitoring tools, and a review of the incident management process. Your team shifts from a build-time mindset to overseeing the managed service, using the established dashboards to track value and performance rather than day-to-day technical execution.

Upon go-live, the immediate focus shifts to establishing a performance baseline. This isn't just model accuracy; it includes key operational metrics like 95th percentile API response time, system throughput (transactions per second), and compute cost per inference. These baselines, captured over the first 72 hours of live traffic, become the Service Level Objectives (SLOs) for the managed service agreement. Any deviation from these baselines post-handover will trigger a severity-based incident response protocol. The managed service team uses a dedicated dashboard monitoring these SLOs in real-time, ensuring they can meet the contractual obligations for system availability and performance from the very first day.

The Critical Role of the Joint Delivery Team

A successful first 90 days hinges on a clearly defined joint delivery team structure. Your side must appoint a dedicated business product owner with decision-making authority and a technical lead from your IT organisation. These individuals are accountable for providing requirements, unblocking access, and making timely decisions to keep velocity high.

Our forward-deployed team typically comprises a delivery lead, two data engineers, and a machine learning engineer. The delivery lead manages the project cadence and stakeholder communication, while the engineers focus on building the system. This team remains consistent from pilot through to the handover, ensuring continuity and deep context.

The team operates on a structured cadence. Daily stand-ups address immediate blockers, weekly review demos show progress to a broader stakeholder group, and fortnightly governance meetings make strategic decisions. This rhythm creates predictability and ensures alignment, preventing the project from veering off course or becoming mired in misunderstandings.

The Joint Delivery Team's effectiveness hinges on a clear RACI matrix for all major decisions. For example, the decision to proceed to UAT is a 'Consult' (C) for the lead data scientist, 'Accountable' (A) for the client's project sponsor, 'Responsible' (R) for the delivery lead, and 'Informed' (I) for the security team. This structure eliminates ambiguity. Weekly governance meetings should review this RACI alongside a project health dashboard that uses a traffic light system (Red/Amber/Green) for areas like data quality, budget burn, and timeline adherence, forcing objective, data-driven conversations about project status.

Key Artefacts and Decision Gates for Governance

Governance is operationalised through specific artefacts and decision gates. The first gate is at day 30, where the ratified data pipeline spec and project charter are reviewed. Approval here confirms the project's technical feasibility and allows the significant engineering effort of weeks 3-6 to proceed with confidence.

The second major gate occurs around day 60, centred on the preliminary business case. This document quantifies the observed benefits from testing, outlines the total cost of ownership for the managed service, and presents a clear return on investment calculation. Its approval releases funding for the production deployment and the managed service contract.

The final gate at day 90 is the operational readiness review. This meeting reviews the results of user acceptance testing, the completed runbook, the security review, and the established performance baselines. Sign-off here formally concludes the build phase and transitions the system fully to the managed service operating model, marking the project's first major success.

A key decision gate artefact is the Model Bias and Fairness Assessment report. This report, generated before the Week 8 integration gate, uses techniques like disaggregated analysis to evaluate model performance across sensitive subgroups (e.g., by geographic region or customer segment). The governance board must pre-define acceptable fairness thresholds; for instance, 'the false positive rate must not vary by more than 5% across subgroups.' If the report shows a breach, the gate cannot be passed, and the team must either mitigate the bias or secure a formal, documented exemption from the ethics committee and project sponsor, attaching that waiver to the artefact.

From Project to Program: Laying the Groundwork for Scale

The true value of a disciplined first 90 days is its function as a blueprint for scaling AI across the enterprise. The processes, artefacts, and governance model proven here become a template for subsequent AI initiatives. This repeatable model reduces the time and risk for future projects, moving your organisation from ad-hoc exploration to systematic execution.

The established managed service agreement provides a proven operational model for maintaining and improving the AI asset. It defines how new data sources are incorporated, how model retraining is triggered, and how enhancements are scoped and delivered. This turns a one-off project into a continuously evolving business capability.

Ultimately, this initial investment creates an internal centre of excellence by example. Your team gains firsthand experience in what successful AI delivery requires, from data engineering to change management. This cultivated expertise allows you to manage more complex AI programmes internally, using external partners like us for strategic augmentation rather than full delivery.

The final weeks must produce a Scalability Assessment document. This technical blueprint outlines the infrastructure changes required to handle 10x the initial transaction volume. It details the necessary upgrades, such as moving from a single Azure instance to a Kubernetes cluster with auto-scaling, and the associated cost impact. Furthermore, it includes a dependency map for the next three use cases, identifying shared data assets and potential bottlenecks. This transitions the conversation from a one-off project cost to a program-level infrastructure investment, enabling the client to make strategic budgetary decisions for the upcoming fiscal year based on concrete technical requirements.
