Yes, you can keep deploying AI after 2 August 2025 — but the ground rules changed that day. The EU AI Act's transparency obligations for general-purpose AI (GPAI) models took effect, turning AI copyright from a fair-use debate for lawyers into a documentation requirement for every enterprise that buys, builds, or fine-tunes models. The new compliance test is blunt: can your providers show what their models trained on, can you show what you trained on, and can you prove what your AI answers are grounded in? Organizations that treat those three records as engineering artifacts, not legal memos, keep their AI programs running while competitors wait.

The Regulatory Landscape in Mid-2025

AI lawmaking is compounding at a measurable pace. The Stanford AI Index 2025 counts a single AI-related legislative proposal worldwide in 2016, more than 150 by 2023, and 131 AI-related bills passed across 46 jurisdictions in 2024 alone; the same report puts global private AI investment at $252.3 billion in 2024. Copyright sits at the center because it reaches into every generative AI deployment: what was trained on, who owns the output, and who is liable when a model built on third-party data produces something your business sells.

Three poles define the mid-2025 landscape. The European Union treats training transparency as a legal obligation: the AI Act's GPAI obligations began applying on 2 August 2025, requiring providers to publish a sufficiently detailed summary of their training content. The United States remains in litigation-led flux: the Copyright Office's January 2025 report concluded that purely AI-generated output without meaningful human control is not copyrightable, while human-directed AI-assisted work can be, and cases such as The New York Times v. OpenAI and Getty Images v. Stability AI (settled in the US in May 2025) keep testing whether training on copyrighted works is fair use. China regulates through explicit administrative rules — the 2023 Interim Measures for Generative AI and the National Copyright Administration's draft measures — making documentation effectively mandatory.

Why Must Your AI Vendors Now Show Their Work?

Because the August milestone gives you a legal hook to ask — and the answer determines how much risk you are carrying. The AI Act's GPAI obligations apply to any provider whose models are used in the EU, wherever the provider is based, and they include publishing a sufficiently detailed summary of training content, keeping internal documentation, and adopting a copyright policy. Non-compliance is expensive: the Act's penalty tiers reach up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover for GPAI transparency breaches — and up to €35 million or 7% for the most serious violations elsewhere in the Act.

Scale matters because training corpora are enormous. OpenAI's own GPT-3 paper documented a pre-filtering corpus of 45 terabytes of compressed text — effectively a snapshot of the public internet that no provider can describe with precision. The "sufficiently detailed summary" requirement was designed around that reality: it asks for a representative overview of training-data sources, not a byte-level audit. When you deploy a GPAI model, you inherit the obligation to verify that summary exists; if a vendor cannot produce it, you are left unable to answer a regulator's first question.

Key Compliance Requirements

  1. Training-data transparency. Know what your model providers trained on and what they will disclose in writing; the EU obligation gives you a standing right to ask, and filing the answer is the cheapest due diligence available.
  2. Output provenance and human control. Because the US Copyright Office ties copyrightability to human creative control, enterprises claiming rights in AI-assisted content must document the human contribution — prompts, selection, editing — before the work is monetized.
  3. Protection of your own data. Proprietary documents fed into public AI tools have repeatedly ended up in training corpora, so the emerging standard keeps confidential data out of any tool without a contractual commitment not to train on it.
  4. Contractual flow-downs. AI software, API, and data licenses now carry copyright representations, indemnities, and training-data exclusions that procurement and legal must review with the same rigor as any other software contract.

Getting any of these wrong is expensive in ways that rarely make a headline. IBM's Cost of a Data Breach Report 2024 puts the global average breach at $4.88 million, but the comparably costly failure is quieter: an AI deployment whose output your organization does not actually own the rights to use, discovered only at the moment of a licensing deal or an audit.

Cross-Jurisdictional Challenges

The hardest part is that one AI system is governed by incompatible rules simultaneously. What is lawful training in Japan — whose 2018 copyright amendment permits non-expressive use of works for data analysis, including AI training — can be squarely contested in the United States and subject to transparency duties in the EU. The United Kingdom has signaled it will legislate transparency and remuneration; Singapore carved out a computational data-analysis exception that makes it one of the most permissive AI jurisdictions; Australia, Canada, and Brazil sit at various unresolved points along the spectrum.

For a multinational, the task is not choosing the friendliest regime but operating one AI program that satisfies the strictest regime it touches: EU-grade transparency globally, human-in-the-loop documentation wherever you claim rights in output, and confidential data routed only to tools with contractual training-data protection. The stakes are compounded by employee behavior: Gartner has warned that by 2027, 40% of AI-related privacy, security, and legal issues will be caused by improper handling of data and models by employees using AI. A casual paste of confidential material into a public tool can trigger simultaneous violations in three jurisdictions.

What Actually Changed on 2 August 2025?

The AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, but its rules were phased. The milestone on 2 August 2025 is the application of GPAI model obligations: providers must now publish a sufficiently detailed summary of training content, keep internal documentation, and maintain copyright-relevant policies. Because the Act reaches any provider whose models are used in the EU, wherever based, the obligation is effectively global. Three practical consequences follow:

  • Vendor diligence becomes mandatory. Your model providers must be able to point to their training-content summaries; if they cannot, you are carrying the transparency risk downstream.
  • Your own training inputs need records. If your organization fine-tunes models on its own corpora, the training-set inventory, licenses, and rights-clearing records are now compliance artifacts, not nice-to-haves.
  • Internal AI-use policies need teeth. Confidential data must stay out of tools without contractual training-data protection — and enforcement, not a policy page, is what protects the enterprise.

None of this requires stopping AI use. It requires the documentation layer most organizations have not yet built — a tractable engineering problem, not a legal wait.

Implementation Strategies

Organizations navigating the 2025 wave treat it as an engineering and procurement problem, in four steps: build the AI asset inventory (every model, API, and AI-enabled product, with provider, training-data disclosures, and contracts); create a training-data record for anything the enterprise trains or fine-tunes itself; formalize human-in-the-loop documentation for AI-assisted creative and commercial work; and update procurement to require vendors to warrant training-data transparency and exclude your confidential data from training.

The commercial pressure to move quickly is immense. McKinsey's May 2025 State of AI survey found 78% of organizations using AI in at least one business function, and IDC projects worldwide AI spending will reach $632 billion by 2028. Enterprises that reconcile that pressure with the new obligations discover that documentation is not a compliance cost but a competitive asset: a documented AI program passes audits, withstands rightsholder inquiries, and qualifies for deals that require proof of lawful training. Those that defer are not avoiding the cost — they are postponing it to the moment of an audit or lawsuit, when it is far more expensive and outside their control.

How Should Enterprises Respond This Quarter?

The response plan for the rest of 2025 is short. Inventory the AI you actually use and assign an owner per tool. Request and file the training-data summaries from your model providers — the EU requirement gives you a standing right to ask. Separate data into "safe for public AI" and "never leaves the perimeter," and enforce that boundary with contracts and access controls, not a policy page. Stand up documentation for any fine-tuning or monetized AI-assisted output. Run it all as a quarterly review: the US is litigating its answer, the EU is implementing its answer, and the rest of the world is watching both.

Preparing for the Next Wave of Regulation

The 2 August 2025 milestone is not the end of the copyright story; it is the opening of its implementation phase. Expect EU enforcement guidance and the first GPAI transparency assessments in late 2025 and 2026, at least one major US training-data ruling in the same window, and more jurisdictions converting consultation into legislation. The enterprises that will be ready treat documentation as infrastructure: an inventory kept current, training records maintained, contracts enforced, and an AI operation whose every answer can be traced. That posture does not guarantee a favorable legal outcome, but it guarantees meeting every regulator and court with evidence rather than hope.
