The short answer: alternative data — utility payments, rental history, telecom usage, and cash-flow signals — combined with AI models can score millions of adults that traditional credit bureaus cannot see, while actually improving default prediction. For lenders this is both an inclusion story and a growth story; for the unscored it is often the first path to formal credit.

Credit scoring with alternative data and AI is one of the most important shifts in Financial Services today, and it is no longer experimental. The question is no longer whether the data works, but how to deploy it responsibly, profitably, and at scale.

Why it matters

The scale of the problem is hard to overstate. The World Bank's Global Findex database has documented that roughly 1.4 billion adults worldwide remain unbanked, and a significant share of those who do hold accounts still have no credit history at any bureau. In many emerging markets, fewer than half of adults have a formal credit file. Traditional scoring models, built on bureau data, simply cannot evaluate this population — so lenders either reject them or push them toward high-cost, unregulated credit.

Alternative data changes the calculus. Utility and rent payments, telecom top-ups, mobile-money transaction history, and bank cash-flow patterns are reliable predictors of repayment behaviour even when a credit file is thin or empty. Industry studies consistently report that lenders using alternative data increase approval rates by 15–25% while keeping delinquency levels flat or lower — because the models see genuine repayment capacity that the bureau cannot see. In credit markets where default costs are measured in basis points of the portfolio, that is a material improvement in both reach and risk.

The competitive implication is equally direct. Lenders that master alternative-data scoring acquire customers competitors cannot price, expand into underserved segments with acceptable risk, and shorten decision times from days to minutes. As regulators in the EU, China, and across APAC tighten rules on both AI and financial inclusion, the ability to score responsibly with alternative data is becoming a license-to-operate issue, not just a growth lever.

It is worth being precise about the opportunity size. In markets where bureau coverage is thin, alternative-data models are not a marginal improvement — they are the difference between having a credit market and not having one. Mobile-money ecosystems in Africa and South Asia, for example, hold transaction histories on hundreds of millions of users who have never touched a bureau file. Lenders that build scoring on those histories are effectively creating a new asset class of creditworthy borrowers, and the first movers set the pricing and risk standards their competitors must match.

Common challenges

The first challenge is data governance and consent. Alternative data is personal data, and using it triggers GDPR in Europe, PIPL in China, and a growing patchwork of fair-lending and data-protection rules elsewhere. Lenders need clear legal bases, transparent consent flows, and supplier agreements that survive regulatory scrutiny. Teams routinely underestimate how much of the project is legal and compliance work rather than modelling work.

The second challenge is bias and explainability. Alternative data can encode discrimination indirectly — a model trained on telecom behaviour can proxy for income, geography, or ethnicity in ways that violate fair-lending obligations. Regulators increasingly require adverse-action explanations: when a customer is declined, the lender must be able to say why, in terms a human can review. A black-box model that scores beautifully on AUC but cannot explain a single decline is not deployable in most jurisdictions.

The third challenge is data quality and drift. Utility and telecom data arrives with missing months, duplicate accounts, and inconsistent formats across providers. Models trained on it drift as providers change products or as customer behaviour shifts after a shock such as a pandemic or an economic downturn. Without monitoring, a model that performed well at launch can degrade quietly within two or three quarters. The fourth challenge is vendor lock-in: many alternative-data providers sell scored outputs rather than raw signals, which limits a lender's ability to own, audit, and retrain its models.

A fifth challenge deserves attention in cross-border markets: data residency and transfer. Alternative data often crosses jurisdictions — a regional telecom group, a global mobile-money platform — and rules on where personal data can be stored and processed vary sharply between markets. A scoring model that works beautifully on data hosted in one country can be unlawful if the same data is processed elsewhere. Lenders operating across borders need a residency map of their data sources as much as they need a model risk framework.

How to get started

Start with a well-defined segment and a clear decision. A pilot on thin-file applicants for a small, unsecured product — a mobile wallet credit line or a two-wheeled-vehicle loan — gives you a manageable risk envelope and a measurable outcome. Define the baseline: what is the current approval rate, default rate, and decision time for that segment, and what would success look like?

Then bring the data in at the signal level, not the score level. Raw cash-flow features — recurring income patterns, spending stability, buffer balances — give your team ownership of the model and the ability to audit it. Build a feature store so signals are computed once and reused across products, and stand up drift monitoring from the first month so the model's performance is visible before it becomes a portfolio problem.

Finally, design the human and regulatory interfaces early. Every model decision must map to an explainable adverse-action reason. Beehive Strategy works with lenders on exactly this pattern: governed feature pipelines, explainable scoring output, and conversational interfaces that let credit officers interrogate a model's reasoning in plain language — so the business can act on the model with confidence rather than treating it as a black box.

Which alternative data sources are worth evaluating first?

Not all alternative data is created equal, and the right starting point depends on your market and your customer base. In practice, cash-flow data from bank and mobile-money transactions is the strongest first move because it directly observes repayment capacity rather than inferring it. Rental and utility payment history follows, especially where rent-to-income ratios are meaningful and landlords report to recognised registries.

Telecom data — prepaid top-up regularity, account tenure, and usage patterns — is valuable in markets where mobile penetration exceeds bank penetration, but it must be handled carefully for bias. E-commerce and social data should be treated with the most caution: they are often noisy, privacy-sensitive, and harder to defend before a regulator or a fair-lending audit.

  • Bank and mobile-money cash flow: strongest predictive signal, direct observation of capacity
  • Rental and utility payment history: strong in markets with formalised rent and bill reporting
  • Telecom usage and top-up patterns: useful in mobile-first markets, watch for indirect bias
  • E-commerce behaviour: promising but noisy, privacy-heavy, and regulator-sensitive
  • Device and psychometric data: highest scrutiny, weakest defensibility in most jurisdictions

The practical guidance is to score the sources on three criteria before piloting: predictive lift over bureau-only models, regulatory defensibility, and data reliability across providers. A source that scores brilliantly but cannot be explained or sustained is a liability dressed up as an asset.

The sequencing also matters commercially. Pilot on a segment where approval uplift is visible fast — thin-file applicants with two to three years of mobile-money history, for example — and where default risk is contained by product design, such as low-limit revolving credit. That combination produces a defensible business case in a few months, which in turn funds the harder work: the governance framework, the explainability tooling, and the monitoring that allow the model to scale across products without a regulatory surprise.

Key takeaways

Alternative-data scoring is a durable competitive shift, not a niche experiment. The lenders that win will be the ones that combine predictive power with governance and explainability from day one.

  • Alternative data can raise approval rates by 15–25% on thin-file segments without raising defaults.
  • Treat consent, bias, and adverse-action explainability as core requirements, not afterthoughts.
  • Buy raw signals and build your own features so you own, audit, and retrain the model.
  • Monitor drift from launch — models degrade quietly without it.
  • Start with cash-flow data on a small, well-defined product, then expand deliberately.
