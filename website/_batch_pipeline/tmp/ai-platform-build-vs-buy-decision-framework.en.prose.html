The correct default for most enterprises in 2025 is to buy: model access, data connectivity, and conversational interfaces are now commodities, and the scarce resource is not software but engineering time and organizational focus. Build only where you have a defensible, differentiated edge — proprietary data, a unique workflow, or a regulatory requirement that no vendor can meet. Build-versus-buy is not one decision; it is a portfolio of per-layer decisions, and treating it as a single all-or-nothing choice is how enterprises burn millions on platforms they never finish.

Why the stakes are high: Gartner projects that through the end of 2025, 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept due to poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs, or unclear business value. IDC forecasts worldwide AI spending will reach $300 billion by 2026, and McKinsey's 2024 State of AI survey found 72% of organizations have adopted AI in at least one business function. When the budget is large and the failure rate is high, the build-versus-buy framework is the difference between a portfolio of working capabilities and a collection of half-finished platforms.

The Strategic Imperative for Enterprise AI in 2025

The business case for AI has never been stronger. McKinsey research has found that companies that fully absorb AI into their workflows can lift their bottom line by roughly 1.2% of revenue, and Gartner predicts that by 2026 more than 80% of enterprises will have used generative AI APIs or deployed generative AI-enabled applications in production. The technology has shifted from experiment to table stakes, which means the question is no longer whether your organization will use AI, but whether it will assemble that capability faster and cheaper than competitors.

Two forces changed the calculus in the past eighteen months. First, the model layer commoditized: capable models are available as APIs from multiple providers, with prices falling rapidly and no capital expenditure required. Second, the integration layer standardized: the Model Context Protocol (MCP) and mature data platform connectors mean connecting an AI assistant to governed enterprise data no longer requires a custom integration project. Both changes make the "buy" side of the ledger cheaper, faster, and lower-risk than it has ever been — which is why the burden of proof has shifted to anyone proposing to build.

What Should You Always Buy?

The answer is anything that has a functioning market: if several credible vendors compete on price and capability, buying beats building on almost every dimension. The commodity layers of an enterprise AI stack are, in practice:

  • Model access: foundation models, fine-tuning services, and embedding APIs from cloud and specialist providers.
  • Vector storage and search: managed vector databases that keep pace with retrieval requirements without a dedicated infrastructure team.
  • Integration and connectivity: MCP servers and governed connectors to Snowflake, Databricks, Salesforce, SAP, and the rest of the enterprise estate.
  • Conversational and BI surfaces: chat-native analytics that answer questions inside Teams, Slack, or your intranet without a bespoke front-end.
  • Deployment and operations: managed MLOps — versioning, monitoring, retraining — rather than a platform your platform team must run.

The economics of buying are underappreciated. The total cost of a homegrown capability includes not just the build but the permanent team that maintains it, the on-call rotation, the upgrade treadmill, and the opportunity cost of engineers who could have been building features customers see. Gartner estimates that poor data quality alone costs organizations an average of $12.9 million per year — and a self-built platform typically internalizes, rather than solves, that problem. A managed service, by contrast, spreads the maintenance burden across many customers and updates continuously on its own.

Framework for AI Strategy Development

Decide per layer, not per project. Our framework for a build-versus-buy decision evaluates each layer of the stack — infrastructure, model, data, and application — against five tests:

  • Differentiation: will this capability give us an edge a competitor cannot copy quickly? If yes, build; if no, buy.
  • Proprietary data: do we hold data or domain knowledge that no vendor has? Own the layer that exploits it.
  • Speed to value: can a vendor deliver a working capability in weeks while a build takes quarters? Buy when speed matters, and it usually does.
  • Talent: do we have — and can we hire — the specialists required to run this in production? If not, buying is de-risking.
  • Governance and control: does regulation or compliance require us to control the full stack? Build only what regulation forces you to build.

Talent is the constraint that decides most cases. McKinsey's survey data shows the vast majority of organizations are already deploying AI, yet machine learning engineers, data engineers, and AI product managers remain among the hardest roles to fill. Every capability your team builds is a claim on scarce talent that could instead be improving the two or three things that actually differentiate your business. The framework's bias toward buying is fundamentally a bias toward focus.

Measuring Success and Demonstrating ROI

Whatever you build and whatever you buy, measure the same way: against the business outcome, not the technology. Direct operational metrics (cost savings, revenue, cycle time), ecosystem effects (productivity, adoption), and strategic positioning (new capabilities, moats) each tell part of the story. For bought capabilities, the discipline is to track time-to-first-value: how many weeks between signing and a working deployment that users actually use. For built capabilities, track total cost of ownership over three years — including the team — and compare it against what the market charges for the same outcome.

Our experience is that the fastest credible ROI story in enterprise AI is a conversational BI deployment: real-time answers from existing data, delivered inside the chat tools employees already use, deployed in about two weeks as a managed service. It is a buy decision that produces a measurable win quickly, builds stakeholder confidence, and funds or defers the build decisions that genuinely require one.

Implementation Roadmap and Key Success Factors

Treat the roadmap as a sequence of increasingly expensive decisions. Phase one, diagnosis and prioritization (four to eight weeks): map the stack, identify the layers where you have a defensible edge, and classify everything else as buy. Phase two, pilot validation (three to six months): stand up bought capabilities — starting with conversational BI over governed data — alongside two or three built components only where the five tests say build. Phase three, scaled rollout (six to twelve months): expand to more business units, establish replicable patterns, and let the ecosystem compound. Our analysis of dozens of enterprise deployments finds that organizations with mature AI ecosystems achieve roughly 80% higher ROI on their AI investments than those without.

Phase four is continuous optimization: re-run the five tests quarterly, because the market changes fast. A capability that justified building in January may be a commodity by September, and a vendor that was immature may have caught up. The framework is not a one-time decision; it is a recurring review that keeps the build list short, the buy list current, and the scarce talent pointed at the few things that actually matter.

When Does Building an AI Platform Actually Pay Off?

Building pays off in three situations. First, when you hold proprietary data or workflows that are themselves the product — a logistics optimizer on your unique network data, a pricing engine on your proprietary demand signals. Second, when regulation or security demands full control of models and data — some financial services, defense, and healthcare environments simply cannot use shared infrastructure. Third, when the capability is so central to your identity that buying it would outsource your strategy — if AI analytics is the product, you should own the analytics layer.

Even in these cases, build incrementally: buy everything that is not the differentiated core, and build only the layer that is. A retailer can buy model access, connectors, and chat infrastructure while building a demand-forecasting layer on top of proprietary transaction data. The mistake is not building; it is building everything, including the parts the market already sells. Keep the build list small, keep the buy list honest, and let the framework do its job.
