Industry Transformation Through AI in 2025

Retail entered 2025 with more channels than ever — stores, e-commerce, marketplaces, social commerce, apps, and messaging — and a customer who moves across all of them as a single journey. The transformation is not about adding channels; it is about unifying the view across them. McKinsey's research on omnichannel behaviour has consistently found that customers who engage across channels are worth more than single-channel shoppers, and the HBR study quantifies the loyalty effect: omnichannel customers returned more often and recommended the brand more than their single-channel peers. The prize is therefore not cosmetic consistency — it is measurable incremental revenue from the same customer base.

The challenge is architectural. A typical retailer holds customer, product, inventory, promotion, and fulfilment data across separate systems — POS, e-commerce platform, ERP, loyalty, CRM — with different identifiers, definitions, and update rhythms. The result is that a customer who browses online, buys in store, and returns by courier looks like three different people, and the retailer cannot answer the simplest questions: what is this customer worth, what do they buy, and what should we offer them next? The leaders are solving this with identity resolution, unified metrics, and a semantic layer that gives every system a shared language — and the laggards remain trapped in channel silos that their customers experience as one brand.

  • Identity resolution. Reconciling customers across devices, logins, cards, and store visits into a single, persistent profile.
  • Unified product and inventory. One view of product definitions, availability, and allocation across stores, warehouses, and online.
  • Cross-channel promotion. Consistent offers and pricing logic that respects channel economics instead of fragmenting them.
  • Unified fulfilment. Buy-online-pick-up-in-store, ship-from-store, and returns treated as one network, not separate operations.
  • Shared loyalty and service. Points, history, and service context that follow the customer across every touchpoint.

The pattern is consistent: unified data is only valuable when the people who make decisions can interrogate it.

What Does a Unified Customer View Actually Require?

Three things, in order. First, identity resolution: a defensible method for deciding when two records are the same customer, including privacy-compliant handling of the personal data involved. Second, shared definitions: what counts as a customer, a purchase, a return, and a channel must mean the same thing in every system, which is exactly what a semantic layer provides. Third, event-level integration: the unified view must update with every interaction — a store transaction, a cart abandonment, a return — or it degrades into a stale report with a modern name. Retailers that attempt unification without all three usually end up with a dashboard that reconciles beautifully and decides nothing.

How Do You Unify Without Rebuilding the Warehouse?

The warehouse rebuild is the most common reason omnichannel programmes stall — a multi-year, multi-million-dollar project that freezes everything else while the channel landscape keeps moving. The alternative is a virtual unification layer: keep the source systems, connect them with standardised connectors, and put a semantic layer on top that resolves identities, unifies definitions, and serves every channel with the same facts. The data never has to move or be copied to be unified for querying, which means the programme starts delivering value in weeks instead of years and can evolve as the channel mix changes.

The operational payoff is immediate. With a unified view, inventory can be allocated across channels against real demand instead of per-channel forecasts; promotions can be consistent without destroying channel economics; and returns can be routed to the cheapest, fastest fulfilment node. The strategic payoff compounds: every channel acts on the same customer truth, so personalisation improves everywhere at once, and the retailer can finally measure what an omnichannel customer is worth — and invest accordingly. The leaders are not building bigger warehouses; they are making the data they already have answer more questions, faster.

Governance is the discipline that keeps unification from becoming a liability. A unified customer view concentrates more personal data in one logical place, which raises the stakes on consent, access, and deletion — the retailer must know who can see what, and must be able to answer a customer's data request across every source in the unified view. The leaders design permissions into the semantic layer itself: role-based access to customer, transaction, and inventory data enforced at query time, with every answer traceable to its sources. That turns the privacy conversation from a project risk into a design feature — and it is the difference between a unified view the business can use and one the legal team cannot approve.
