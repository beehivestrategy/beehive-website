Synthetic data is the most practical answer to the tension between AI ambition and data privacy, because it lets teams build and test models without exposing sensitive records. Gartner projected that by 2024, 60% of the data used for AI and analytics projects would be synthetically generated, and the trend has only accelerated since. For enterprises across Asia-Pacific — where data protection regimes such as China's PIPL, the EU GDPR, and sectoral rules in banking and healthcare converge — synthetic data turns the compliance bottleneck into an engineering advantage. This article explains what synthetic data can and cannot do, where it fails, and how to operationalise it safely.

The Current Landscape

Enterprise adoption of synthetic data has moved from academic experimentation to production infrastructure in a remarkably short window. The drivers are concrete. Privacy regulation makes it costly to move real customer data between regions, into third-party testing environments, or into model training pipelines at all. At the same time, real datasets are frequently too small, too imbalanced, or too incomplete to train reliable models — a fraud model trained only on the handful of genuine fraud cases a bank records in a quarter will simply never see enough examples of the behaviour it needs to detect.

Synthetic data addresses both problems at once. Generators — ranging from rule-based systems and statistical copulas to generative adversarial networks, diffusion models, and LLM-based tabular generators — produce artificial records that mirror the distributions, correlations, and edge cases of the original data without containing any individual's actual records. The market has responded accordingly: industry estimates put the global synthetic data market at roughly USD 1.3 billion in 2023, with projections in the range of USD 4 billion to USD 5 billion by 2028, a compound growth rate of over 30%.

The use cases now span every sector we work with. Financial services generate synthetic transaction streams to test fraud detection and anti-money-laundering models without exposing account data. Healthcare organisations share synthetic patient cohorts for research while keeping real clinical records internal. Retailers simulate demand and pricing scenarios that have never occurred historically. The common thread is that synthetic data changes the risk calculus: teams can experiment, iterate, and fail fast in a safe environment, then promote only validated models to production.

Key Implementation Challenges

The first challenge is the fidelity-versus-privacy trade-off, and it is not optional to confront. A synthetic dataset that reproduces the original records with near-perfect fidelity is, by definition, close enough to allow re-identification or membership inference — an attacker can determine whether a specific person's data was in the training set. A dataset engineered purely for privacy may become so generic that it no longer reflects the real-world patterns the model must learn. Organisations that ignore this tension end up with synthetic data that is either a privacy risk dressed up as a solution, or a model that performs poorly in production.

The second challenge is validation. There is no point generating synthetic data if you cannot prove it is representative. Teams need measurable evidence across three dimensions: statistical fidelity (do distributions, correlations, and ranges match?), privacy risk (how resistant is the output to re-identification?), and downstream utility (does a model trained on synthetic data perform acceptably against real data?). In our assessments, fewer than one in three organisations that start generating synthetic data have formal validation gates in place when they begin — most add them only after a model trained on synthetic data fails its first production evaluation.

The third challenge is governance and lineage. Synthetic datasets are derived assets, and they need the same treatment as any other data product: clear ownership, documented generation parameters, versioning, retention rules, and an audit trail back to the real source data. Regulators are watching this space closely. The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, with the bulk of obligations applying from 2 August 2026, and both it and the emerging PIPL implementation guidelines treat training-data provenance as a first-class compliance question. Synthetic data can be the answer to those questions — but only if its provenance is documented as rigorously as the real data's.

How Do You Know Synthetic Data Is Safe to Trust?

Trust in synthetic data comes from measurement, not from the reputation of the generator. The practical answer is to institutionalise a three-gate validation protocol before any synthetic dataset is released to model training or analytics. Gate one checks fidelity: compare the synthetic distribution against the real distribution using statistical tests and correlation matrices, and require the gaps to be within agreed tolerances for every field that matters to the use case. Gate two checks privacy: run membership-inference and re-identification probes, and document the measured risk. Gate three checks utility: train a reference model on real data, an equivalent model on synthetic data, and require the synthetic model to land within a defined performance band — commonly within 5% to 10% on the primary metric — before either is trusted in production.

That protocol also answers the operational question of who owns the decision. In the enterprises we support, the data governance committee — not the data science team alone — signs off on releasing a synthetic dataset, because the trade-offs involved are business and compliance decisions as much as technical ones. When validation is treated as a release gate rather than a post-hoc analysis, synthetic data becomes something the risk function can defend and the board can understand, which is precisely the condition under which it scales beyond a single pilot.

Practical Approaches That Work

Start with the use cases where the risk-adjusted return is highest. The clearest wins are the ones that are impossible or very expensive with real data: fraud and financial-crime model testing, analytics over PII-heavy customer records, cross-border and third-party data sharing, and stress-testing systems against rare events. A bank can generate a synthetic dataset containing thousands of fraud patterns — including ones never seen in its own history — and validate its detection models against that far richer test bed. A retailer can model promotional scenarios that have never occurred, rather than waiting for a real-world experiment.

Match the generator to the data. Tabular data with complex correlations responds well to diffusion-based and LLM-based generators, while time-series and geospatial data have specialised generators that preserve temporal and spatial structure. Treat generation as a repeatable pipeline step, not a one-off script: the generator should be versioned, parameterised, and executed on a schedule, with every run recorded in the data catalogue alongside lineage back to the source data it derives from.

Integrate synthetic data into the analytics workflow rather than creating a parallel one. The teams that succeed treat synthetic datasets as first-class citizens of their semantic layer: business users can query a synthetic sandbox in natural language, explore scenarios, and interrogate the assumptions — while every answer remains traceable to a documented, validated dataset. Key Takeaways

Five principles separate organisations that use synthetic data as a genuine capability from those that merely claim to. The principles are simple to state and demanding to execute.

  • Measure trust, don't assume it. Every synthetic dataset should pass explicit fidelity, privacy, and utility gates before release.
  • Target high-value, high-risk use cases first. Fraud, PII-heavy analytics, cross-border sharing, and rare-event simulation deliver the clearest return.
  • Document provenance like real data. Version generators, log parameters, and keep lineage from synthetic output back to source.
  • Make governance the release authority. Data governance owns sign-off; data science proposes, governance disposes.
  • Integrate, don't isolate. Synthetic sandboxes should be queryable through the same semantic layer and conversational interface as production data.

Conclusion

Synthetic data generation is not a replacement for real data — it is a complement that changes where and how fast teams can learn. Enterprises that embed generation, validation, and governance into a repeatable pipeline gain the ability to train and test AI systems safely, share data across borders without breaching privacy rules, and simulate futures that have not happened yet. Those are capabilities their slower-moving competitors will find very difficult to copy.

The organisations that succeed combine rigorous validation with pragmatic use-case selection, and they treat synthetic data as a governed data product rather than a data science side-project.
