China's deep synthesis rules are no longer a niche compliance footnote — as of September 1, 2025, every AI-generated or synthetic image, video, audio, and text distributed in China must carry explicit and implicit labels under the CAC's new labeling measures. Combined with the Deep Synthesis Provisions that took effect in January 2023 and the Generative AI Interim Measures of August 2023, this gives enterprises a complete, enforceable labeling regime to design for. This article breaks down what the 2025 updates change, what multinational teams must do differently, and how to operationalize compliance without rebuilding your content pipeline.

The Regulatory Landscape in Mid-2025

The Chinese regulatory framework for synthetic content has built up in three deliberate layers. The first layer is the Provisions on the Administration of Deep Synthesis Internet Information Services — the Deep Synthesis Provisions — which took effect on January 10, 2023. They require providers and users of deep synthesis services to label synthetic content, obtain consent from individuals whose likeness is used, and prevent misuse for fraud, defamation, and election manipulation. The second layer is the Interim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services, effective August 15, 2023, which extended the rules to generative AI broadly and required providers to label content generated by AI. The third layer arrived in March 2025, when the Cyberspace Administration of China published the Measures for the Labeling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content — effective September 1, 2025 — which introduce a dual-track system: explicit labels that users can see, plus implicit labels embedded as metadata that technical systems can detect.

The significance of the 2025 measures is that they close the enforcement gap. Earlier rules required labeling "in accordance with relevant provisions," but did not specify the technical mechanism; the new measures standardize it. Social media platforms rushed to comply before the September 2025 effective date, and international reporting — including SCMP's coverage of platforms adopting AI-content labeling — documented the scramble. For enterprises, the practical effect is that labeling is no longer a publishing decision; it is a pipeline capability.

Key Compliance Requirements

For enterprises operating in China or distributing content to Chinese users, the compliance surface is broader than most teams expect. The core obligations, in order of operational impact:

  • Dual-track labeling. AI-generated content must carry a visible label (for example, text prompts or watermarks) and an implicit label embedded in the file's metadata, so that platforms and regulators can verify provenance algorithmically.
  • Real-name management. Deep synthesis service providers must verify the real identity of users who generate or publish synthetic content, per the 2023 Deep Synthesis Provisions.
  • Consent and likeness protection. Using a person's face, voice, or likeness requires informed consent; the provisions explicitly target non-consensual impersonation and fabricated video.
  • Record-keeping. Providers must log who generated what, when, and with which model, and retain those records for a defined period so that provenance can be reconstructed after the fact.
  • Content moderation. Synthetic content must not be used to spread false information, disrupt financial markets, or interfere with elections and public order.

The design intent is visible across all three layers: China is treating AI-generated content as a systemic information-integrity risk, and the regulations are written to make the origin of every piece of synthetic content auditable. That is a fundamentally different posture from regimes that regulate only the most harmful deepfakes — it applies to marketing images, product renders, voice agents, and synthetic training data alike.

How Do the New Labeling Rules Affect Enterprise AI Content?

For most multinational enterprises, the September 2025 measures change three concrete workflows. First, creative and marketing teams that generate images or video with AI must tag those assets at creation, in the asset management system, so that the label travels with the file into any Chinese-market distribution channel. Second, product teams shipping AI features into China — voice assistants, image editors, translation tools — must implement labeling at the generation endpoint, not as a manual QA step. Third, procurement teams buying AI-generated content from agencies must contract for labeling compliance, because the publisher is liable, and the publisher's liability flows to whoever commissioned the content.

The cross-jurisdictional complication is that these obligations overlap with the European Union's AI Act transparency rules, which require disclosure when people interact with AI systems and labeling of certain AI-generated content, with fines up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover for the most serious violations. A multinational that builds labeling once, as metadata attached at generation time, satisfies both regimes; a company that treats China labeling as a local patch will be rebuilding it every time a new market passes a transparency rule.

Cross-Jurisdictional Challenges

The hardest part of Chinese synthetic-content compliance is not reading the rules — it is knowing what your organization actually generates and where it travels. That inventory problem is where most programs stall. Synthetic assets are produced by marketing teams, product teams, support teams, and increasingly by employees using consumer AI tools that are invisible to IT. If you do not know which assets are AI-generated, you cannot label them, and if you cannot prove labeling, you are exposed on every distribution channel.

This is also where enforcement history matters. The CAC's July 2022 fine of Didi — ¥8.026 billion, roughly US$1.2 billion, for violations of the Cybersecurity Law, Data Security Law, and Personal Information Protection Law — demonstrated that Chinese authorities will impose headline-making penalties when they find systemic non-compliance. The labeling measures are the latest example of the same enforcement posture: clear rules, technical requirements, and the willingness to inspect. Enterprises should assume that platform audits and targeted inspections will test whether labels are actually present in metadata, not just whether a policy document exists.

Implementation Strategies

A pragmatic implementation sequence starts with an asset inventory: scan the systems where marketing, product, and customer-facing content is produced and stored, and identify where AI generation is already happening — including shadow AI use. Next, instrument the generation endpoints: the highest-leverage fix is adding mandatory labeling metadata at the point of generation in the tools your teams already use, rather than post-hoc labeling that will be forgotten under deadline pressure. Then standardize the label format to the CAC's requirements and verify with automated checks that a sample of exported assets contains both visible and implicit labels. Finally, extend the same discipline to third-party content: contract clauses requiring suppliers to label AI-generated deliverables, plus spot checks on delivery.

Two operating lessons from teams that have shipped this. First, treat labeling as a data problem, not a legal problem: the deliverable is a metadata schema, an inventory, and an audit trail — all of which can be monitored continuously. Second, make compliance status visible to the people who ship content: when a content operations lead can ask, in plain language, "what share of our Chinese-market assets are labeled and verified?" and get a real-time answer, labeling stops being an annual scramble and becomes part of normal operations. Preparing for the Next Wave of Regulation

China's synthetic-content regime is not static. The September 2025 labeling measures are the current milestone, but the trajectory is toward deeper provenance requirements — model registration, training-data disclosure, and cross-border transfers of synthetic content are all under active regulatory discussion, and the EU AI Act's general-purpose AI transparency obligations continue to apply from August 2025 onward. The enterprises that will be resilient are those that treat labeling and provenance as infrastructure rather than projects: a metadata standard attached at generation, an inventory that is continuously maintained, and monitoring that makes compliance measurable at any moment. Build that infrastructure now, and the next wave of regulation becomes a configuration change rather than a program of work. The foundation you build today determines whether 2026 is a year of compliance anxiety or a year of shipping AI content with confidence.
